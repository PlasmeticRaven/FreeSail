**Units.** Forfait's and Lescallier's *pied* is the pied du roi, 0.3248 m (1.066 English feet; the
tree's `VesselReferences-2026-10.md` §0), of 12 *pouces* (2.707 cm) of 12 *lignes* (2.256 mm); a
square pied is 0.1055 m². Chapman's Swedish foot is 0.2969 m. Winfield's Spanish feet are **pies de
Burgos**, 0.2786 m, and the game takes Winfield's own metres (the Spanish note's head). Every figure below
is given in its source's units first and in metres after, the metres this note's arithmetic. A
French *tonneau* of burden is a capacity measure, not a displacement; the figures are kept as the
sources give them (that a tonneau de mer is 42 cubic pieds, about 1.44 m³, or 2,000 livres, is
memory, unverified).

**In short.**

- **The rig the owner settled is the period's tartane, found in four period texts and a table.**
  Röding (vol. II, TARTANE) gives one mast with a lateen "over which there is usually a topsail",
  a staysail forward, galley's runner-tackle shrouds, and two-masted tartanes too. Forfait 1788
  (pp. 123 to 125, and Table II, p. 134, **read on the page image**) gives the *tartane simple*
  (the lateen with a square topsail over it, set as a sloop's) and the larger tartane with a lateen
  on a mizen stepped near the stern, "which the Levantines call *misaine*", with a topsail and
  sometimes a topgallant; and in the ocean, the great lateen exchanged for a square sail of the
  same area and two or three jibs. Lescallier 1791 (pp. 400 to 401) and Steel 1794 give the single
  mast, the lateen, one jib, and the square *tréou* set before the wind or in too much wind.
- **Two named tartanes have their masts and yards in a period table** (Forfait Table II): the
  *Espiègle*, 75 tonneaux, a tartane simple, 51¾ by 18 pieds (16.81 by 5.85 m); and the *Humble*,
  100 tonneaux, with topsail and mizen, 62¾ by 20⅓ pieds (20.38 by 6.61 m), her main mast 59
  pieds (19.17 m), her lateen yard 75 pieds (24.36 m), her mizen 30 pieds (9.75 m) with a lateen yard
  of 47 (15.27 m), her sail 2,237½ square pieds (236 m²). The *Humble* is the owner's rig to the
  sail.
- **One plan of the type exists in the period: Chapman 1768 Pl. LVIII No. 16, a French tartane
  for war**, 62¼ by 17 7/12 Swedish feet, drawing 6⅔ (18.48 by 5.22 m, 1.98 m), eight 4-pounders and
  four swivels (read there, `VesselReferences-2026-10.md` §3.1). Her lines are the only lines; the
  plate was not reached at a readable size in this round.
- **The Spanish navy kept seven tartanas**, mostly bought or chartered from French builders; they
  served now and then as coastguard and look-out vessels; the rig is Monleón's (Winfield pp. 1249
  to 1252, the lead's words). Their names, sizes, guns and men are the passages to ask for.
- **The handling is the lateen's: she goes about either way, her yard changed round the mast or
  left.**
  - **The period texts** give the yard changed from side to side "pour virer de bord", going about
    either way, by easing the column shrouds (Lescallier p. 378, *trelucher* or *muder
    l'antenne*). They warn that a lateen taken aback in a strong wind is the loss of the mast or
    the vessel (Forfait p. 119). They name the board sailed with the yard left on the mast
    (Stratico 1813, whose figure for it is a tartane; O'Scanlan 1831: GA §2.2).
  - **The modern record** of Provençal practice (a 2018 heritage inventory citing Vence 1897, small
    craft) has the lateen boat change her yard by a wear "when the weather allows", the yard
    brought up beside the mast and yard and sail passed round to the other side. It has her avoid
    long boards on the wrong hand, where the sail lies pressed against the mast. It does not say
    she could not tack.
  - **No source read says a lateen vessel could not tack** (GA §4). The owner's answer to question
    3 (2026-11-18) has her tack and wear as other vessels do, the yard's change preferred and not
    necessary: their knowledge, said as JUDGEMENT. **Plainly:** the first two drafts' "worn, not
    tacked" was the brief's, read here as agreeing with the inventory; the inventory does not
    say it.
- **The engine today, run on the lead's `tartane_probe`:** she classes as the cutter; she *tacks*
  in 89 s with nothing done to either lateen (no dip, no side, no cost); she wears in 487 s with the
  yards never shifted; hove to she lies 54° off at 3.1 knots with both lateens drawing; `dip the
  mainsail` is refused. What she needs is in her design (§5 there).

## 1. The type and the name

- **Falconer 1780, TARTAN** (read, OCR): a small coasting vessel of the Mediterranean, one mast and
  a bowsprit, "the principal sail, which is extremely large, being extended by a lateen yard".
- **Steel 1794 I, TARTAN** (read, OCR): a small vessel of the Mediterranean coast, one mast and a
  bowsprit; "A large latteen-sail is carried on the mast, similar to a xebec, and a large jib
  forwards. When the wind is aft, a square-sail is" set (the verb lost to the OCR) "like a
  cross-jack."
- **Lescallier 1791 pp. 400 to 401** (read, OCR): one mast *à calcet* carrying a lateen like a
  galley's, rigged the same, with column shrouds; a jib forward, made fast at the end of the
  *berthelot* or *flèche*; used in the Mediterranean for trade, chiefly in Provence, from which
  some are sent to the ocean ports and even to America. "Les Tartanes peuvent aller, soit avec le
  vent arrière, soit avec un vent trop fort, en gréant une voile quarrée, appelée tréou."
- **Röding vol. II, TARTANE** (read, OCR): the words in eight languages (Spanish and Portuguese
  *tartana*); a small vessel in common use in the Mediterranean (Fig. 289); "Die Takelasche
  desselben besteht aus einem Mast mit einem lateinischen Segel über dem sich gewöhnlich noch ein
  Toppsegel befindet", forward a kind of staysail; the mast's shrouds runner tackles as a galley's;
  **"Es giebt auch zweymastige Tartanen."**
- **Forfait 1788 pp. 123 to 125** (read, OCR; the page numbers by the running heads, whose tens
  digit the OCR garbles from p. 119 to p. 127, p. 134 checked on the page image):
  - add a square topsail above the lateen of the one-masted lateen *barque* (Forfait's fig. 35), set "as
    the sloops' is" (fig. 9), and one has the **tartanes simples**: coasters very numerous in
    the Mediterranean, used by every nation of it from port to port; by them Toulon is supplied
    and the wines and other produce of Provence carried; they sometimes pass the Strait, but go no
    further into the ocean than Cadiz without the precautions given next;
  - they are canvassed at most as the least-canvassed sloops, with the centre of effort much
    higher; the same number of yards; lighter gear, "well compensated by their want of strength";
  - **when the breadth passes 19 to 20 pieds and the length 60**, the single lateen grows too
    great: the main sail is reduced and a like sail set on a mast stepped near the stern as a
    *tapecul*, which "the Levantines call *misaine*"; sometimes a topsail and a topgallant are
    added; the whole a greater sail, handier for its division than the tartane simple's, but
    having lost the merit of simplicity;
  - **the tartanes that go into the ocean**, as far as the Channel in summer, keep that rig but
    exchange the great lateen for a square sail of the same area, with **two or three jibs on the
    éperon**; only the mizen is left of the lateen rig; even so their qualities are middling, they
    cannot bear bad weather, and they would go about very badly were not their relative breadth
    always very great.
- **Forfait p. 125, the éperon** (read, OCR): the Mediterranean vessel's beak, a projection of
  timber forward of the stem, in place of the bowsprit; it serves to tack the forward sails, and is
  sometimes lengthened by a wooden *flèche* laid over it, the **berthelot**, which takes the stays'
  standing parts and the jibs' tacks. Lescallier p. 393 (read, OCR): lateen vessels have no
  bowsprit nor jib-boom, only the flèche or berthelot projecting forward, near the horizontal, its
  end also the point for the forward ourse of the yard.
- **Winfield pp. 1249 to 1252** (the lead's words): small coasting craft, almost all in trade, now
  and then taken up as coastguard vessels for reconnaissance; a large mainmast and a very small
  mizen; the type common across the Mediterranean under other flags but little favoured in Spain;
  seven tartanas known in the navy's service, three bought or chartered from French builders and
  two from a private builder in Spain.
- **The period's other uses of the word.** Falconer's CAGOUILLE (read, OCR): the scroll at the
  prow of polacres, xebecs and tartans, Falconer's Pl. XII fig. 12: a carving for her head (principle 9:
  words, not a drawn figure, unless the plate shows it).

**What is known:** a Mediterranean coaster of Provence above all, of one mast or two, under a
great lateen with a square topsail over it in the type's usual form, a jib or more on the beak, a
square tréou for running and for too much wind; common in every Mediterranean nation's trade and in
the King's service of France and Spain now and then. **Judgement:** the owner's rig is the larger,
two-masted tartane of Forfait's second column and Röding's last sentence, not Lescallier's and
Steel's one-masted vessel.

### 2.2 Chapman's French tartane (read there, `VesselReferences-2026-10.md` §3.1)

Pl. LVIII No. 16, "A French tartane (Vessels of war)": 62¼ Swedish feet between perpendiculars
(18.48 m), breadth moulded 17 7/12 (5.22 m), draught 6⅔ (1.98 m); eight 4-pounders, four swivels;
"used in the Mediterranean both to carry merchandise and as a fighting ship" (the reprint's
gloss). Length to breadth 3.54, against the *Espiègle*'s 2.88 and the *Humble*'s 3.09: a fine

## 3. The rig, mast by mast

### 3.1 The main mast

- **A mast *à calcet*:** short and stout, its head a square block (the *calcet*) pierced with
  sheaves for the halyards; no stays; shrouds of the column kind (Lescallier pp. 376 to 377, read,
  OCR). The *Humble*'s: 59 pieds, 22 pouces in diameter, 26 pieds of head (§2.1).
- **Column shrouds** (*haubans à colonne*): a pendant at the masthead with a single block at its
  foot, a runner through it from a ringbolt on the gunwale, set up by a tackle to a second ringbolt;
  their use is that **they are cast off easily when the sail is to be changed to the other side in
  going about**, "ce qu'on appelle trelucher, ou muder l'antenne" (Lescallier pp. 377 to 378, read,
  OCR). Röding: runner tackles, as a galley's (read, OCR). Galley shrouds (*sartis*) are the other
  kind (Lescallier p. 377); the tartane's are the column's (p. 400).
- **Its rake.** Decision 11 says "one raking mast". The texts read give no rake for a tartane's
  main; Forfait's xebec has the fore mast raked forward and the mizen aft, "these opposite rakes
  spreading the sails over a greater length" (p. 128, read, OCR). The watercolour's main stands
  near upright, its mizen raked well aft (seen). **Open** (§9).
- **The great lateen yard** (*antenne*): of two pieces of fir laid together for most of their
  length, long and very thin, bound by woolding and iron hoops, thickest where it meets the mast,
  tapering to a point at the top; slung by a parrel (*racage*, *chapelet*) at about a third of its
  length; much longer than the mast and rising far above it; the top end the *penne*, the lower the
  *car* (Lescallier pp. 378 to 379, read, OCR). The *Humble*'s: 75 pieds, 9¼ pouces (§2.1),
  **1.195 of her length**: the yard is longer than the vessel. Forfait p. 123: the lateen yard's
  diameter is relatively half that of a lug yard or a square yard (read, OCR).
- **Its gear** (Lescallier p. 379, read, OCR): worked at the lower end, to windward, by **two
  ourses** like a mizen's, the *ourse à proue* and the *ourse à poupe*; the top, to leeward, by a
  **brace** (*oste*) on a pendant; a second brace, the **vang** (*van*), on a pendant and fall,
  steadies the yard and takes its strain; the forward ourse leads to the berthelot's end (p. 393).
  The halyard (*drisse*) through the calcet's sheave (Forfait p. 121).
- **The topsail**, square, on a yard hoisted on the masthead above the lateen (Forfait; Röding;
  Winfield's Monleón; the watercolour). The *Humble*'s yard 32 pieds (10.39 m); the *Espiègle*'s 27
  (8.77 m), over a square yard of 41 (13.32 m). A topgallant yard of 24½ in the *Humble* only.
- **The tréou** (Lescallier pp. 395, 401; Falconer TREOU, *voile de fortune*, SQUARE-SAIL; Steel):
  a square sail set "with the wind aft or too strong", "like a cross-jack" (Steel). Falconer's
  SQUARE-SAIL: "only used in fair winds, or to scud under in a tempest", with a bonnet in fair
  weather taken off to scud. On the *Espiègle*'s 41-pied yard, or on a yard sent up for it
  (JUDGEMENT; the cutter's square sail is set so in the game).

### 3.2 The mizen

- Forfait's *tapecul*, the Levantine *misaine* (pp. 124 to 125): a mast near the stern with a lateen
  "semblable" to the main's, smaller; the *Humble*'s 30 pieds with a 6-pied head, its yard 47 pieds
  (15.27 m), stepped 15 pieds 2 pouces abaft the middle (4.93 m).
- **Its purpose**, Lescallier p. 393 (read, OCR): the moderns added a mizen to some lateen vessels
  "to serve to bring them to the wind", which they needed once they left off going by the oar when
  the wind was foul: the mizen is a **driver**, a sail to bring her head up.
- The watercolour's mizen rakes well aft, its yard nearly upright, its sheet led to a spar over the
  stern (an outrigger, seen); Forfait's xebec's after mast "panché vers l'arrière" (p. 128).
- Winfield (the lead's words): Monleón's print shows the "diminutive mizen" of the custom.

### 3.3 The head

- **The éperon and the berthelot** (§1): the *Humble*'s éperon projects 10½ pieds (3.41 m), the
  *Espiègle*'s 10 (3.25 m). Steel and Falconer say "bowsprit"; the watercolour shows a long spar
  near the horizontal, as Lescallier's flèche is (seen).
- **The headsails, one or two.** Lescallier: "un foc" (p. 401). Steel: "a large jib". Röding: "a kind
  of staysail or stay-foresail". Forfait: two or three jibs on the éperon in the ocean rig (p. 124).
  The images README and the Spanish note read two in the watercolour; this note's reading of it
  sees one great jib to the berthelot's end and cannot make out a second at 800 px (seen). The
  owner's question 3 (§10).

## 4. The handling

### 4.1 Setting, trimming and taking in

- **Trim** (Lescallier p. 379, read, OCR): the yard is worked at its lower end on the weather side
  by the two ourses and at its top on the lee side by the brace and vang; the sheet aft and to
  leeward "as the wind is more or less oblique to her course".
- **Taken in**: "Les voiles latines se carguent d'ailleurs comme les autres voiles" (Lescallier
  p. 379): clewed up to the yard; furled on it by its *matafions* (gaskets, p. 380). The yard's
  woolding is the step to climb to the peak (Falconer, YARD, `Research-Bilander-2026-10.md` §1.1).
  The 2018 inventory (secondary) gives the furl from the deck by its brails, by hand on small boats,
  and with buntlines (*embrouilles*) on vessels over ten metres.
- **Peaking for a calm aloft** (Forfait p. 120, read, OCR): in the narrow channels of the Archipelago
  or under the land, with no air at the water and a current of it aloft, the penne is raised by
  hauling down the car with its ourses, carrying part of the sail into the upper air; the same
  movement raises or lowers the centre of effort. A lateen's own trim the square rig has not.
- **Reefed** by one band "placé obliquement" (Lescallier p. 379); the 2018 inventory: the sail
  lowered to a man's height to tie its reef points, "a very delicate manoeuvre".
- **Changed for the weather**: the galley's great sail exchanged for a larger (*marabout*, *grande
  bourde*) in light winds and a smaller (*bouffette*) when the wind is too strong (Lescallier
  pp. 394 to 395, read, OCR); Falconer's XEBEC the same with three suits; Forfait p. 129, the lateen
  craft's lateens unbent in a bad sea and a forced wind and two square sails of half the area set
  in their place (read, OCR).

### 4.2 Going about: tacked and worn, the yard changed or left (third draft)

- **The yard changed.** Lescallier p. 378 (read, OCR): the column shrouds are cast off "when the
  sail is to be changed to the other side in going about", called *trelucher* or *muder l'antenne*.
  "Virer de bord" covers tacking and wearing both; which is meant is not said. Röding vol. I col. 655
  (read, OCR): *changer, muder, trelucher* are the French of a fore-and-aft sail gybing; the
  Spanish *tomar por la pena*, "to take it by the peak".
- **The danger of being taken aback.** Forfait p. 119 (read, OCR): when lateen sails are taken aback
  ("coiffées", "font chapelle") in a strong wind the mast or the vessel is lost, the canvas wrapping
  the mast so that the yard cannot be lowered; and (p. 120) the yard, very long and weak, would
  infallibly be broken by the first squall if she had to hold the wind in a storm to claw off a lee
  shore.
  Fincham 1843 p. 59 (read there, `VesselReferences-2026-10.md` §3.1): by the wind the lateen is
  "more liable to be taken aback than the common lug".
- **The wear and the shift, as practised** (PCI 2018, secondary, citing Vence 1897 and Huet,
  Rigaud and Vigne; paraphrased): a lateen boat avoids long boards on the "bad hand" (*a bido*, the
  mast to leeward, the sail pressed against it) and, when the weather allows, at going about wears
  and passes yard and sail to the other side of the mast: during the bear-away the sail is hoisted
  close up and the parrel eased; a hand forward takes the car and brings it to the mast while the
  sail flies like a flag; with the yard upright beside the mast the helmsman passes the sheet round
  forward of the mast; the sail set on the new side, the parrel hauled taut, she luffs up on the
  good hand. Running on the bad hand the sail may shift of itself (*la trélouche*); better to shift it
  first. The search's summary of the same source, that tartanes could not stay and so wore, is the
  search tool's gloss and is not counted. The sheet itself never says a lateen boat cannot tack;
  its "when the weather allows" implies times she did not wear to change the yard (GA §2.4, §3.2).
- **The yard left, and the tack** (third draft; GA §2.2, §3.1):
  - Stratico 1813 (BISDOSSO) has a lateen vessel close-hauled go about "prontamente" with the yard
    left on the mast, so as not to have to change it. Only in a vessel of one sail and in fine
    weather, "altrimenti, sarebbe pericoloso". Its figure is a tartane.
  - O'Scanlan 1831 (VELA) has lateen vessels hold the new board without changing the yard.
  - Romme 1792 and Willaumez 1831 name that board, *à bidot*, *bido*.
  - James has two settees and an armed xebec "tacked" in British officers' accounts (B).
- **Wearing in general**: Forfait p. 127 on the galley before the wind, her two lateens goose-winged
  (*en ciseaux*; the Levant's *oreilles de lièvre*, Lescallier p. 395), half the area of other rigs
  on the same course (read, OCR).
- **What is known** (rung A):
  - the yard is shifted at going about, either way;
  - the yard may be left and the new board sailed on the mast, a prompt going about in fine weather;
  - a lateen taken aback in a strong wind is a grave danger;
  - she lies better on one hand than the other.
- **What is reported** (rung B, late, small craft): when the weather allowed she changed her yard
  by a wear, the yard stood up beside the mast and passed round while she ran off, and she kept
  her boards on the bad hand short.
- **What is judgement:**
  - that a 100-tonneau tartane of 1800 changed her 24-metre yard in the same way, with more hands
    and the ourses and halyard instead of a man's arms;
  - by the owner's answer to question 3, that she tacked and wore as other vessels did, her yard
    changed in stays or in the wear, or left.

  How her design builds it, and what each choice costs her in the game, is
  `Design-Ship-Tartane.md` §5.4 items 2 to 5 and 11 (measured on her probe:
  `Design-Package90-Engine.md` §2.2a).

### 4.3 How tartanes sailed

- **Lies a point nearer the wind** than a square-rigger: five points, 56° 15', against six, 67° 30'
  (Lescallier p. 380, read, OCR, the footnote); "Vessels with latteen-sails will lie one point nearer
  the wind than a square-rigged vessel" (Steel 1794 I p. 237, the xebec, read there, Bil §4).
- **Less gear, lighter masting, more easily trimmed** (Lescallier p. 380).
- **Centre of effort high** (Forfait pp. 119, 123): the tartane simple's 8.33 m on a breadth of 5.85
  m (§2.1).
- **Delicate**: the management of such vessels "demands much practice and prudence" (Forfait p. 122,
  said of the one-masted lateen barques, read, OCR).
- **Ocean-going, middling and unable to bear bad weather**, going about badly but for her great
  breadth (Forfait pp. 124 to 125).
- **Good in a moderate breeze and a smooth sea by the wind, the worst of all before the wind in a
  rough sea**: said of the xebec (Forfait p. 129; Fincham p. 59 of the lateen), carried to her
  (JUDGEMENT).

**Truth candidates** (handling rules, for her design's §13):
1. Close-hauled she lies about a point nearer the wind than a brig, at about five points (Lescallier
   p. 380; Steel I p. 237).
2. She tacks and wears; going about, her yards are shifted round their masts in stays or in the
   wear, or left on the bad hand (Lescallier p. 378, the change "pour virer de bord"; Stratico,
   BISDOSSO, and O'Scanlan, VELA, the yard left; the owner's answer to question 3). **Plainly:**
   the second draft's "she does not stay under her lateen" is said by none of the three sources it
   cited (GA §6), and is dropped.
3. On the bad hand her lateen, pressed against the mast, draws less than on the good (PCI 2018; the
   lug's rule in the game, a third off).
4. Before the wind with the tréou set she runs better than under the lateen alone, and the lateen
   is taken in for it (Lescallier p. 401; Steel; Falconer).
5. In a blow the great lateen is taken in or exchanged for a smaller, and she lies to under her
   mizen and a headsail (Lescallier pp. 394 to 395; Forfait p. 129; the lie-to's canvas JUDGEMENT).

## 11. The owner's questions (the pick first)

1. **Her size.** *The pick*: the *Humble*'s 100 tonneaux, 62¾ by 20⅓ pieds (20.38 by 6.61 m), her
   masts and yards from Forfait's Table II without the topgallant, Chapman's lines scaled to her.
   Or the *Espiègle*'s 75 tonneaux with a mizen added (Forfait says a tartane of her size has none),
   or Chapman's war tartane at her own scale.
2. **Going about.** *Answered by the owner, 2026-11-18* (Dec 3, Part F), in their own shape: she
   tacks and wears as other vessels do, her yards shifted round their masts in stays or in the wear
   (the yard stood up by the mast and its car passed round, never lowered: *muder*), or left on
   the bad hand until shifted; the change preferred and not necessary. The pick ("worn, not
   tacked") came from the brief and is dropped.
3. **Her main's rake.** *The pick*: 3° forward, provisional, so that "raking" holds and the
   watercolour's near-upright main still looks right; or upright; or raked further. (The headsails
   are judgement by decision 11 itself: one great jib to the berthelot's end, Lescallier, Steel,
   Röding; the README's two recorded.)
4. **The tréou.** *The pick*: carried, set on a square yard crossed for it as the cutter's square
   sail is, before the wind and in too much wind, out of plain sail. Or left out.
5. **Her three roles in files.** *The pick*: one hull in two files, one class in two roles
   (principle 10, the brig and the merchant brig): the trader (with the fisherman's gear as a fit)
   and the coastguard, whose guns and men differ too much for one company. Or one file whose fits
   change only her guns.
6. **The boat.** *The pick*: towed astern on passage, as the owner's picture has it, hoisted in or
   veered long in a blow. Or carried on deck.
