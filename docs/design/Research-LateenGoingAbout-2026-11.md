**The words.** Every period language has one word for going about either way and two for its ways,
and every word for changing a lateen's yard is tied to the first:

| Language | Going about, either way | Tacking (head to wind) | Wearing (stern to wind) | Changing the lateen's yard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| French | virer de bord | virer vent devant | virer vent arrière | trelucher, muder, gambier; faire le car |
| Spanish | virar de bordo | virar por avante | virar por redondo | cimar; hacer el caro (galleys) |
| Italian | virare di bordo | virare col vento in prua | virare col vento in poppa | treluccare, mutare; fare il carro |
| German | wenden | durch den Wind wenden | vor dem Winde wenden, halsen | durchkaien (the ship's whole mizen) |
| English | go about | tack, stay | wear, veer | change, shift (the mizen) |

(Forfait pp. xxvi to xxvii, l. 800 to 806; Falconer, TO TACK and VEERING; Röding II, WENDEN, l.
99854 to 99960; Stratico, VIRARE, leaves 340 and 513 to 515; O'Scanlan, CAMBIAR, the Archive's
p. 187, for *virada por avante*, and *por redondo* from memory, not looked up; the lateen words
below.) So a text that says the yard is changed "pour virer de bord" says nothing of
which way she went about. The same words are also used of a fore-and-aft sail's gybe, the act of a
wear (Röding I col. 655, GIEPEN: *changer, muder, trelucher*, "en parlant des voiles auriques";
Romme, TRELUCHER, to gybe or shift the sails); said of a lateen's yard, no dictionary read ties them
to the tack or to the wear.

## In short

- **No source read says a lateen vessel could not tack, or that she never did.** The one period
  text that says a vessel with a yard of this kind "never" tacks is of the Bombay *dinga*, a boat
  of 24½ *pieds* (Lescallier pp. 463 to 464: "ne virent jamais que vent arrière"; Steel 1794 I
  p. 241 the same, copied; Fincham 1843 p. 290 softens it to "commonly wear instead of tacking").
  An Indian boat, an analogue only; and the same Lescallier who recorded it for her did not record
  it for the galley, the xebec or the tartane (pp. 376 to 401).
- **For tacking, at rung A:** Stratico 1813 (BISDOSSO, p. 62) has a lateen vessel, the tartane its
  figure, going about "prontamente" from close-hauled and holding the new board with the yard not
  changed, the sail on the mast: done only in a one-sail vessel and in fine weather, "altrimenti,
  sarebbe pericoloso". O'Scanlan 1831 (VELA, "Llevar la vela sobre el palo") has the faluchos and
  other lateen vessels change board and hold the new one without changing the yard, yard and sail
  pressed by the wind against the mast. Romme 1792 (BIDOT) and Willaumez 1831 (BIDO) name that board
  on the mast; Willaumez's is close-hauled and "s'exempter de muder". Röding (col. 578) and the
  seventeenth-century English orders have the ship's whole lateen mizen changed at every going about,
  in the tack as well; Falconer's lateen bean-cod is "well fitted to ply to windward". **At rung
  B:** James has two settees (1807) and an armed xebec (1811) "tacked" in British officers'
  accounts; Fincham 1843 thinks the fore-and-aft sail replaced the lateen as "more convenient in
  tacking".
- **For wearing, or against tacking:** the danger of a lateen taken aback in a strong wind (Forfait
  p. 119; Fincham p. 59); the galley's evolutions "lentes & incertaines" (Forfait p. 127) and her
  oars, not her sails, against a contrary wind (Forfait p. 126; Lescallier pp. 393 to 396; Steel I
  p. 238); Stratico's limit on going about with the yard left; and, at rung B, the 2018 inventory,
  which has the lateen boat choose at going about to change the yard by a wear "si le temps le
  permet", avoiding long boards on the bad hand.
- **What decides it: nothing.** No manoeuvre book of the period read here works a lateen vessel at
  all, no dictionary's word for the change of a lateen's yard picks the tack or the wear (the same
  words mean a fore-and-aft sail's gybe: Röding, GIEPEN; Romme, TRELUCHER), and no account
  was found of a xebec, a galley, a polacre or a tartane in stays or missing stays. **So the owner's
  own knowledge of lateen vessels tacking today stands as the judgement, said as one (JUDGEMENT,
  the owner's, 2026-11-18):** a lateen vessel tacks and wears as other vessels do, and the change of
  the yard round the mast is preferred and not necessary. Nothing read contradicts either half, and
  each half has period text that agrees with it in part (§4).
- **On the costs** (§5): the sources give the danger, the bad hand by its names, the labour and the
  yard's change in words; they give **no figure** of the way or wind she needs to stay, of how much
  less the sail draws on the mast, or of the hands and seconds the change takes. Those are
  JUDGEMENT.

## 3. For and against

### 3.1 That a lateen vessel tacked

1. **Stratico 1813, BISDOSSO (A, in the period).** The nearest the period comes to the owner's
   words. The vessel is close-hauled ("correndo stretti al vento") and goes about "prontamente",
   leaving the yard where it was. The text says *virare il bordo*, either way; but one goes about
   promptly from close-hauled by the tack, and a wear from close-hauled is the slow way (Falconer,
   VEERING; Röding, WENDEN), so the tack is the reading (JUDGEMENT on the reading, a short one). Its
   figure is a tartane.
2. **O'Scanlan 1831, "Llevar la vela sobre el palo" (A, after the period).** Lateen vessels go about
   and hold the new board without changing the yard. Either way.
3. **Romme 1792, BIDOT, and Willaumez 1831, BIDO (A).** The board on the mast has a name, and
   Willaumez's is a close-hauled board run to spare the *muder*. A vessel close-hauled with her sail
   on the mast has come to that board by a tack or by a wear with the yard left; a name for it means
   it was sailed (JUDGEMENT on what the name implies).
4. **The ship's lateen mizen (A for its own rig; analogue).** Röding: the whole mizen had to be
   shifted at every *Wenden*; the English orders for going about close-hauled ended "shift the
   mizen" (Boteler, c. 1634, read there, Bil §1.5). A whole lateen yard was changed in stays as a
   matter of course, on a ship's mizen.
5. **The lugger (A for its own rig; analogue).** A yard-sail that must be changed in going about,
   and still the lugger was put in stays, losing ground when it was often repeated (Steel I p. 221).
6. **James (B).** Two settees (1807) and an armed xebec (1811) "tacked".
7. **Falconer's bean-cod (A)** plies to windward under her lateen; **Fincham 1843 (B for this)**
   judges the lateen less convenient in tacking than the sail that replaced it: inconvenient, not
   impossible.

### 3.2 That she wore, or that the tack was hard

1. **The dinga (A; analogue):** never tacks (Lescallier; Steel), commonly wears (Fincham). An Indian
   boat of 24½ *pieds* whose sail is "à-peu-près comme une voile de bélandre".
2. **Taken aback (A):** a lateen taken aback in a strong wind costs the mast or the vessel (Forfait
   p. 119); more liable to it than a lug (Fincham p. 59). A tack brings the wind ahead; a wear never
   does. This is the period's strongest reason to prefer the wear in a breeze, and it is a danger,
   not a rule.
3. **The galley (A):** her evolutions slow and uncertain (Forfait p. 127); against a contrary wind
   she rowed with her yards on deck (Forfait p. 126; Lescallier p. 396; Steel I p. 238); the mizen
   added to lateen vessels to bring them to the wind once they rowed less (Lescallier pp. 393 to 394;
   Steel I p. 237). A galley tacking under sail is not described, nor forbidden.
4. **Stratico's limit (A):** going about with the yard left only in a one-sail vessel and in fine
   weather. Read strictly, "una sola vela" covers neither the game's tartane (her mizen and her
   topsail) nor the xebec; how far to carry it is JUDGEMENT, for item 2.
5. **The 2018 inventory (B):** the yard changed by a wear when the weather allows. It does not say
   she wore every time she went about, and its "when the weather allows" implies times she did not.

### 3.3 The yard: changed or left

- **Changed, and how (A):** stood up in the mast's axis and the heavy end passed abaft (Willaumez,
  MUDER); the car passed close by the mast and **forward** of it (O'Scanlan, CIMAR, from another
  dictionary); stood up against the mast with the sheet passed forward (the dinga, Lescallier); the
  car brought to the mast and the helmsman round forward of it with the sheet (the 2018 inventory).
  The side the heel passes is not agreed; it matters to the viewer, not to the time.
- **Left (A):** Stratico, O'Scanlan, Willaumez and Romme all name the board with the yard left.
- **When it is changed (none says, except the inventory):** no period text says the lateen main's
  yard was changed in stays rather than in the wear, or the reverse. The ship's mizen was changed in
  stays (Röding; Boteler). The 2018 inventory changes it in a wear (B). Stratico gives the reason to
  leave it: to go about promptly. So the change costs time in the tack (JUDGEMENT on Stratico's
  implication).

### 3.4 What none says

- That a lateen vessel could not tack, or never tacked (but the dinga).
- How a lateen vessel tacks, step by step: no order, no sequence, no officer's words.
- A xebec, a galley, a polacre or a tartane in stays or missing stays: no account found.
- The wind or the way she needs to stay, in knots or in any measure.
- How much less a lateen draws on the bad hand.
- The hands or the time to change a yard of a xebec's or a tartane's length, in stays or in the wear.
- How a polacre with lateen fore and mizen and a square main goes about.

## 4. The conclusion

**Nothing read decides it.** A deciding source would be a manoeuvre book that works a lateen vessel,
or a dictionary that ties the change of the yard to the tack or to the wear; none was found. The
words for the change of a lateen's yard are all tied to going about either way (the same words for
a fore-and-aft sail mean its gybe: Röding, GIEPEN; Romme, TRELUCHER); the manoeuvre books of the period
(Bourdé, Steel's second volume, Chapman, Lever) work ships and say nothing of lateen vessels; the
one "never" is of an Indian boat.

**So the owner's own knowledge of lateen vessels tacking today stands as the judgement, said as one
(JUDGEMENT, the owner's, 2026-11-18): a lateen vessel tacks and wears as other vessels do, each an
ordered evolution, and the change of the yard round the mast is preferred and not necessary.** What
the period text adds:

- It contradicts neither half. "Worn, not tacked" as a rule has no source behind it; it came from
  the package's brief.
- It agrees with the second half in four dictionaries: the board with the yard left has a name in
  French (1792, 1831), Spanish (1831) and Italian (1813), and two of them (Stratico, O'Scanlan)
  describe going about and holding the new board with the yard left.
- It agrees with the first half in part: Stratico's prompt going about from close-hauled reads as a
  tack; British officers' accounts in James have settees and an armed xebec tack; the ship's lateen
  mizen was changed in stays.
- It gives the reasons for the "preferred": the bad hand is the worse board (O'Scanlan, VUELTA), and
  leaving the yard is only for fine weather and a one-sail vessel (Stratico); and the reason to fear
  the tack in a breeze, the lateen taken aback (Forfait p. 119).

## 5. What the tack costs and risks: what the sources give

For items 2 to 4. Where a line says JUDGEMENT, no source read speaks to it; the judgement offered is
this note's, for those documents to take or change.

### 5.1 The way and the wind she needs to stay

- **The sources:** a lateen lies at five points, a square-rigger at six (Lescallier p. 380; Steel I
  p. 237); the mizen was added to lateen vessels to bring and keep them to the wind (Lescallier
  pp. 393 to 394; Steel I p. 237), the after sail that brings her head up in stays; a head sail
  "assists the vessel in going about" (Steel's houario, p. 238); the galley's evolutions slow and
  uncertain for want of divided sail (Forfait p. 127); the tartane re-rigged square would go about
  very badly but for her breadth (Forfait pp. 124 to 125). In general: a ship misses stays for want
  of way, the rudder too weak to carry her past the point where every sail shivers (Steel II p. 316,
  l. 7740 to 7761); a ship ill trimmed or full-bodied will not go about in a head sea (Chapman); in
  a violent wind and sea tacking is impracticable and ships wear (Falconer, VEERING; Röding, WENDEN).
- **JUDGEMENT:** no figure for a lateen vessel. The general rule holds for her: she needs her way;
  the tartane, her one great lateen turned by a jib and a small mizen, is slower and less sure in
  stays than the xebec with her sail divided on three masts (Forfait p. 127's reason, said of the
  galley); Stratico's "con tempo bello" sets the yard-left tack in fine weather and says nothing of
  a tack with the yard changed in a breeze.

### 5.2 Missing stays

- **The sources, in general only:** a ship misses stays when her head will not come up into the wind
  to bring her on the other tack (Steel II, MISS STAYS); she falls back on the board she was on, for
  the officer's error or a high sea beating her bow back, and should then be worn (Stratico, RICUSARE
  DI VIRARE, leaf 404; VIRARE, leaf 515, the second way); box-hauled or club-hauled near a lee shore
  (Steel II pp. 324 to 325).
- **For a lateen vessel: none found. JUDGEMENT:** she misses as any vessel misses, with this of her
  own: if the yard is to be changed in stays and she falls back, the yard is still on its old side
  and on the good hand for the old board, so nothing is lost but ground and time; if she hangs head
  to wind, she is near to being taken aback (§5.3).

### 5.3 Taken aback in a breeze: the sail, the yard and the mast

- **The sources:** in a strong wind, a lateen "coëffée" or making "chapelle" costs the masts or the
  vessel, because the canvas wraps against the mast and the yard cannot be lowered (Forfait p. 119);
  the long weak yard broken by the first squall in a storm (Forfait p. 120); the lateen more liable
  to be taken aback by the wind than the lug (Fincham p. 59), the cutter not liable at all (Fincham
  p. 66); a lugger's large sail dangerous if she makes "chapelle" or takes the wind ahead in heavy
  weather, the smaller sail her safety (Lescallier p. 409); the lateen yards hard to work and liable
  to break without great practice (Stratico, STAMBECCO, p. 437); *chapelle* in general, close-hauled,
  the helmsman lets the sails be taken aback and she comes round head to wind against her will
  (Willaumez); *tomar por avante* the same (O'Scanlan, AVANTE, p. 121); the yard left on the mast
  dangerous but in fine weather (Stratico, BISDOSSO).
- **Which goes first, the yard or the mast, and at what wind: none says. JUDGEMENT offered:** in
  light and moderate airs a lateen taken aback falls off on the other board with her sail on the mast
  (the bad hand), as a *chapelle*; in a strong wind the sail cannot be lowered while aback, and the
  yard and then the mast are at risk, the yard first (Forfait's two pages put the yard's weakness
  and the mast's loss side by side; which first is this note's reading). The wind at which it turns
  from the first to the second is for item 3, measured against the engine's own gale for the lug.

### 5.4 The hands and the time to change a yard of her length, in stays and in the wear

- **The sources:** the xebec's crew has "at least the labour of three square-rigged ships" (Falconer,
  XEBEC, a captain of Algiers); xebecs rigged as polacres to manage their sails more easily than
  lateens (Lescallier pp. 372 to 373; Stratico, STAMBECCO); the ship's whole mizen shifted at every
  going about was "eine beschwerliche Arbeit" and gave way to the half mizen (Röding I col. 494, II
  col. 578); the column shrouds cast off easily for the change (Lescallier p. 378), the yards worked
  round the mast by parrels (Fincham p. 60); the carguette that stands the yard up (Willaumez;
  O'Scanlan p. 652); the lug lowered and hoisted again in going about, which keeps large ships from
  the rig (Forfait p. 6), and the lugger losing ground in stays when the tack is often shifted (Steel
  I p. 221); in a small modern boat the change in the wear takes a hand at the halyard and parrel, a
  hand forward at the car and the helmsman with the sheet, and is not for one man (the 2018 inventory,
  B); the change is left in order to go about promptly (Stratico).
- **No count of hands and no seconds, for any yard. JUDGEMENT:** the change wants all hands in a
  vessel of the xebec's size (the engine's `too_few: all hands`, as A1 drafted); it takes longer in
  stays than in the wear, because in stays it is done while she is head to wind and losing her way,
  and in the wear the sail flies before the wind and the yard can be stood up at leisure (the
  inventory's description and Stratico's reason, read together); the time scales with the yard's
  length and the hands with its weight (the *Humble*'s yard 24.4 m, Tar §2.1 and §9; the xebec's
  and the polacre's by their designs).

### 5.5 The bad hand: the sail against the mast, and how much less it draws

- **The sources:** named *à bidot* (Romme 1792, BIDOT), *bido*, *bideau* (Willaumez), *a bisdosso*
  (Stratico), *mala vuelta*, *falso bordo*, *navegar* or *llevar la vela sobre el palo* (O'Scanlan),
  *a bido* (the 2018 inventory). The wind presses the fore part of the sail against the mast
  (Willaumez; Romme); the sail forms two bellies, one before the mast and one abaft (Stratico); the
  yard and sail are driven by the wind against the mast (O'Scanlan, VELA); the less advantageous
  board (O'Scanlan, VUELTA); harder in heavy weather and the boat roughly used, the foot chafing the
  mast (the inventory, B); before the wind on the bad hand the sail may come over of itself, *la
  trélouche*, and is better shifted first (the inventory, B); *tumbar la vela*, putting it on the good
  board when it is on the mast (O'Scanlan, TUMBAR); Willaumez's MUDER changes the yard so that on
  the other board the sails "ne soient pas chargées sur le mât" (its OCR reads "l'autre bout",
  taken here as *bord*: to check on the page image).
- **How much less it draws: none says. JUDGEMENT:** the game's lug rule (a third of the cloth lost
  against the mast, `physics/sails.py`'s `against_the_mast`, as A1 drafted and the tartane's truth
  candidate 3 has it) is a fair reading of Stratico's two bellies; to be measured on the hulls, not
  sourced. A risk of *la trélouche* running before the wind on the bad hand is the inventory's, and
  late.

### 5.6 The polacre's square main with her lateens

- **The sources:** the polacre's square main, lateen fore and mizen "generally" (Falconer, POLACRE);
  the polacre-settee, "The main-mast ever keeps the rigging of the polacre" (Steel I p. 236; Fincham
  p. 292); the polacres keep their lateen forward while they stay in the Mediterranean (Forfait
  pp. 130 to 131); a square-rigged polacre going about head to wind, plate C fig. 13 (Willaumez,
  POLACRE, read there, Pol §4, the plate not viewed); the Spanish chebec with a square main and
  lateens fore and mizen sails well (Forfait p. 130), its going about not described. Nothing on how
  a polacre's lateens are worked in the tack or the wear.
- **JUDGEMENT** (as Pol §4 has it, and the owner's shape now general): she tacks and wears as a brig
  with her main; her fore lateen is changed or left as the xebec's foremost; her lateen mizen is
  worked as the ship's whole mizen of the old kind, which was changed in stays (Röding; Boteler,
  a century and more before the period), or left on the bad hand for the board.
