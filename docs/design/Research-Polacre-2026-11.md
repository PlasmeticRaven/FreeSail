**In short.**

- **The name covers three rigs in period.** Falconer 1780: three masts, "generally" square on the
  main and lateen on the fore and mizen, some square on all three "particularly those of
  Provence". Steel 1794 splits it: POLACRES (square on three pole masts, a ship's sails) and the
  POLACRE-SETTEE (a lateen fore; square on the main and mizen, or a lateen mizen and square on the
  main alone; "the main-mast ever keeps the rigging of the polacre"). Lescallier 1791 describes
  only the square one. Forfait 1788: in the Mediterranean polacres keep the lateen forward; they
  are square-rigged forward only for the Ocean. **The owner's plan is Falconer's "generally" and
  Steel's second polacre-settee.** By 1831 the Spanish word means mostly a two-masted vessel
  (O'Scanlan: "dos palos tiples", a brig's sails; some three-masted).
- **The pole mast is the rig's mark, and the sources agree on it** (Lescallier pp. 370-372, Steel
  1794 vol. I p. 236, Falconer, Röding, Forfait pp. 131-133, O'Scanlan p. 482, Winfield pp.
  1083-1084): one stick, no tops, caps or crosstrees; bolsters for the eyes of the rigging; a rope
  ladder in place of topmast shrouds; the topsail and topgallant **lowered together down to the
  lower yard** in a squall ("amener en paquet"); the men furl the topsail from the lower yard and
  the topgallant from the topsail yard, the yards lowered for it, and the upper yards carry no
  footropes. Light aloft; but a mast broken aloft means the whole mast unrigged, so pole masts are
  made shorter and thicker, often of two pieces scarfed and hooped.
- **Forfait gives a pole mast rule** (p. 132): the pole's length such that the eyes of every
  shroud fall where a three-piece mast would put them; and the Levant's own rule (p. 133): the
  main pole mast of a three-master so many breadths long (four and a fraction; the fraction lost
  in the OCR); its diameter at the deck 1/60 to 1/62 of its length, the foremast's 1/68; the
  yards' lengths by the ordinary system, their diameters a twentieth less (lower) and a fifteenth
  less (upper). The Mediterranean makes the fore and main pole masts, "jamais celui d'artimon".
- **The lateen is Lescallier's (pp. 376-380) and Fincham's (pp. 59-61):** a yard of two spliced
  pieces slung at a third, worked at its lower end on the weather side by two ourses, at its
  upper end by a brace and a vang, the sail sheeted aft to leeward, one oblique reef, clewed up
  like any sail; shrouds set up as runners, cast off and shifted when the sail is changed round
  the mast to go about ("trelucher, ou muder l'antenne"). A lateen lies at five points, a square
  rig at six.
- **No polacre's dimensions or lines were found.** The hull: Falconer says the xebec's hull is
  "extremely different" from the polacre's (1780); O'Scanlan (1831) that the polacra's is "very
  like" the jabeque's; Forfait that polacres derive from xebecs but rank now as square-rigged
  vessels of burthen. Her size rests on judgement until the owner's Winfield passages come.
- **James gives eight armed polacres of the war with their guns and men** (§6), among them the
  Spanish navy's polacre-ship *San Josef* (1800: fourteen brass guns, a crew of 49 and 22
  soldiers), a Spanish privateer (1801: ten guns pierced for twenty, 94 men), a laden Spanish
  polacre with ten guns and 113 men (1799), and a Tripolitan polacre-ship of fourteen guns and 80
  men (the regency's cruiser).

**Reading** (JUDGEMENT): in the game's years the three-masted polacre with a lateen fore and mizen
and a square pole main is Falconer's "generally" and Steel's second polacre-settee, and Forfait
says the Mediterranean kept the lateen forward. The square polacre of Provence (Lescallier, Steel's
POLACRES, Röding) is the same hull and pole masts square-rigged throughout, a ship's handling: a
fit of the hull at most. The Spanish navy's word of 1831 means a two-masted square vessel; the
navy's eight of 1780 to 1818 may be any of these, which only Winfield's rows can say (§11).

## 2. The pole mast

### 2.1 What it is

- **One stick.** Falconer MAST: a mast "formed of one single piece, which is called a pole-mast"
  (read, OCR). Lescallier p. 370: three masts "d'un seul jet, ou d'une seule pièce, sans hunes,
  chouquets, ni barres de perroquet", and the bowsprit's end ("bout de beaupré") of one piece too
  (read, OCR). Steel p. 236 the same, "a bowsprit of one piece" (read, OCR). O'Scanlan p. 453: the
  *palo macho, enterizo ó tiple*, the mast of one piece; p. 576, TIPLE, adj., said of a mast of one
  piece (read, OCR, online).
- **Built of two or three pieces.** Lescallier p. 372: often of two pieces, joined by a long scarf
  at the eyes of the rigging, strengthened by iron hoops and rope woldings (*cercles de fer &
  roustures*) (read, OCR). Winfield: two or three poles scarfed and held by iron rings (the lead's
  words). Forfait p. 133: where the pole is longer than the pines it is made of, two masts are
  scarfed end to end (read, OCR).
- **The mizen sometimes not.** Lescallier p. 371 "excepté quelquefois celui d'artimon"; Steel "The
  mizen-mast is sometimes not in one piece"; Röding "den Besahnmast zuweilen ausgenommen" (read,
  OCR). Forfait p. 133: the Mediterranean's square-sailed vessels make the fore and main masts
  pole masts, "jamais celui d'artimon" (read, OCR).

### 2.2 Its rigging

- **Bolsters, no tops.** At the proper height a *baderne* and a *bourrelet* are fixed to take the
  eyes of the shrouds, stays, the pendants of the great tackles, the burtons (*caliornes*) and the
  *candelettes* (Lescallier pp. 370-371, read, OCR); Steel: "bolsters fixed, as a stop to the
  shrouds, stays, &c." (read, OCR); Röding: *Flechtingen* or a wreath and *Serwingen* (read, OCR).
- **What it lacks.** No top rope (*guinderesse*: there is no topmast to sway), no catharpins
  (*trelingage*), no futtock shrouds (*gambes de hune*), no topmast or topgallant shrouds; in their
  place **a rope ladder with wooden rungs on each side of the mast from its head down to the eyes
  of the lower rigging** (Lescallier p. 371; Steel; Röding's *Sturmleiter*) (read, OCR).
- **The yards' footropes.** Steel: all the yards "except the lower yards, are without horses"
  (read, OCR); Falconer: "neither have they any horses to their yards" (read, OCR). The sources
  differ on the lower yards (§10).

### 2.3 Its handling: the yards lowered

- **Handing in a squall.** Lescallier p. 371: the topsails and topgallants, "n'ayant rien qui
  arrête leur descente le long du mât, s'amènent toutes deux jusques sur la vergue basse, ce qui
  est avantageux pour amener promptement dans une surprise de vent, ce qu'on appelle amener en
  paquet" (read, OCR). Röding: both lowered to the lowest yard, "bey einem plötzlichen Windstoss
  von grossem Nutzen" (read, OCR). O'Scanlan p. 482: the upper sails lowered lie in the lee of the
  lower sails and are furled easily (read, OCR, online).
- **Furling and reefing from the yard below.** Falconer: "the men stand upon the top-sail-yard to
  loose or furl the top-gallant-sail, and on the lower-yard to reef, loose, or furl the top-sail,
  whose yard is lowered sufficiently down for that purpose" (read, OCR). Steel p. 236: "they stand
  upon the lower yards to loose or furl the topsails, and upon the topsail-yards to loose or furl
  [the topgallant sails], as the yards are easily lowered for that purpose" (read, OCR).
- **The lead's reading** (`VesselReferences-2026-11-Spanish.md` §2): the square sails of a polacre
  handed from the deck by lowering the yards, "a thing the generator does not yet say". The
  sources say the yards are lowered to the yard below and the men work from that yard: aloft
  still, but on the lower yard and without a climb to a top (JUDGEMENT on the reading).
- **Light aloft, handier.** Lescallier p. 371: the rigging very light, the tops, caps and much
  weight being suppressed (read, OCR); Winfield: less weight aloft, the ship handier (the lead's
  words).

### 2.4 Its costs

- Lescallier p. 372: if a pole mast breaks aloft the whole mast must be unrigged and taken out to
  repair it, where a broken topmast or topgallant mast of the three-piece rig is soon replaced at
  sea by a spare; so pole masts are made **less tall**, given **an excess of diameter**, and of the
  best wood (read, OCR).
- Forfait p. 131: the masts' accidents are irreparable, and the work is harder and wants livelier
  seamen, "parce qu'il n'y a aucun repos depuis le pont jusqu'à la girouette" (read, OCR).
- Winfield: a mast less able to stand a strong wind, which kept the rig in the Mediterranean (the
  lead's words). Forfait p. 131: polacres in the Ocean, "quelquefois & même dans la Manche, mais
  toujours avec désavantage" (read, OCR).

### 2.5 Its proportions (Forfait pp. 131-133, rung A as a rule, read (OCR))

- **The heights.** "Les dimensions, la disposition & la répartition des voiles, doivent toujours
  être les mêmes" with pole masts as with the three-piece rig; the pole's length is such that the
  eyes of all the shrouds fall at the heights they would have in the system it replaces. The book's
  example is the King's aviso of its Table 9 masted à pible. As the OCR reads it: lower mast 58
  pieds, main topmast 31½, topgallant mast 14, "de la somme 114" less the lower masthead 7, the
  topmast head 4 and the topgallant's pole 6, leaving 97 pieds (31.5 m) from the mast's foot to the
  topgallant shrouds' eye; the pole 103 pieds (33.5 m), with 6 pieds (1.95 m) of pole above that
  eye; the lower shrouds' eye at 51 pieds (16.6 m); the topmast shrouds' at 79½ pieds (25.8 m), from
  a sum of the lower mast and topmast printed 90½. **Plainly:** the OCR's figures do not close (58,
  31½ and 14 make 103½, not 114; 58 and 31½ make 89½, not 90½): a topmast length misread, most
  likely. To read on the page image before the rule's example is used; the rule itself is plain.
- **The Levant's rule.** The ports of the Levant hold it "une loi constante" to give the main pole
  mast of a three-master a length of four times and a fraction the vessel's breadth, and four and a
  fraction a brigantine's; the fractions are lost in the OCR ("4 fois & * la largeur", "4 fois ;").
  The book's checks: the flûte *la Tampone* (Table 5) 105 pieds (34.1 m), the aviso 104 pieds (33.8 m),
  "ce qui s'accorde assez" with the general rule. To read on the page image before any figure is
  taken from it.
- **The diameters.** At the deck, the main pole mast 1/60 to 1/62 of its length, the fore 1/68;
  the yards' lengths by each system's proportions, their diameters a little less, a twentieth for
  the lower yards and a fifteenth for the upper.
- **Lescallier against Forfait** (§10): Lescallier makes pole masts less tall than the three-piece
  rig; Forfait puts their eyes at the three-piece heights.

## 3. The lateens

### 3.1 The yard and its gear (Lescallier pp. 376-380, read (OCR); Fincham pp. 59-60, read (OCR))

### 3.2 The fore lateen and the fore mast

- **Raked forward.** Monleón's print (the lead's words); the xebec's fore "incliné sur l'avant"
  (Lescallier p. 398, read there: `VesselReferences-2026-10.md` §3.3); "a fore-mast raking

- **Its tack on the bowsprit.** In the Levant the name *polacre* is given to a lateen at the fore

  (BIGON; read, OCR). These are of small craft; for the polacre's fore lateen they are JUDGEMENT.
- **Its size.** Fincham's xebec (Ex. 4, 125 by 32 ft): the fore lateen yard 0.9 of the main's and

### 3.4 How near the wind

- Lescallier p. 380: a lateen vessel lies at five points (56° 15' between the keel and the wind),
  a square-rigged at six (67° 30'), and the lateen has far fewer ropes, a far lighter mast and is
  trimmed more easily (read, OCR). O'Scanlan, CUARTA (the Archive's p. 246): lateen and
  fore-and-aft vessels sail at a smaller angle than the usual six points (read, OCR, online,
  through the search's snippet).
- Steel 1794 vol. I p. 237: "Vessels with latteen-sails will lie one point nearer the wind than a
  square-rigged vessel" (read there, `Research-Bilander-2026-10.md` §4).
- Forfait p. 130, the Spanish xebec with a square main: she does not hold the wind as near as one
  all under lateens, "mais ils boulinent mieux encore que la plupart des autres Navires", sails
  well large and passably before the wind, rises well to the sea and pitches gently, her bow less
  loaded than a vessel's with a square fore and a bowsprit (read, OCR). The polacre's case is the
  reverse of that xebec's (a lateen fore, a square main), so this is JUDGEMENT for her.
- Fincham p. 59: the lateen "possesses advantages in light winds, when close-hauled"; before the
  wind in a rough sea "the most disadvantageous of all sails"; by the wind "more liable to be taken
  aback than the common lug" (read, OCR).

## 7. Winfield's facts (pp. 1083-1084; the lead's words, rung B)

- Spanish classification by rig, so a polacra could be a brig-schooner, a corvette or a jabeque by
  hull.
- **Eight in the navy's records between about 1780 and 1818.** Their names, dimensions, guns and
  men are not in the lead's note.
- The pole mast: one spar without separate topmast and topgallant, two or three poles scarfed and
  held by iron rings, so no tops or crosstrees; less weight aloft and a handier ship; the yards
  lowered and raised without the tops in the way; a mast less able to stand a strong wind, which
  kept the rig in the Mediterranean.
- Monleón's print (p. 1084; cited, not held): a lateen on a forward-raked foremast, square sail on
  a pole mainmast (course, topsail, topgallant), a lateen mizen, the jibs on a long bowsprit.
- **Units:** a Spanish row's feet are pies de Burgos (0.2786 m); the game takes Winfield's metres.
