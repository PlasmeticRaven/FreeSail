# Research note: a 74-gun ship for package 75 (October 2026)

Phase 1 of the ships workflow (`Plan-ShipsWorkflow.md` §2) for the fourth ship of package 75:
"a 74, or a ship of the line of a like class" (`docs/dev/M7-WorkPackages.md`, Package 75,
item 3). The owner's word of 2026-10-28: the research starts from the *Bellona* or the
*Bellerophon*, may propose a similar class found more interesting, with its reason, and the
owner decides from it. This is the one document the design and the build work from for the
74, and the one the owner chooses the class from.

Two research rounds were run in five parts (the candidates, the hull, the guns and people,
the rig, the engine), each re-read or re-measured by an independent checker, with a critic's
list of gaps between the rounds. Where a researcher and a checker differ, this note gives the
checker's figure and the checker's access, and says so. It reuses the methods and findings of
`Research-Indefatigable-2026-10.md` (the same designer's 64, measured the same way; cited
"Ind") and `Research-ShipsEngine-2026-10.md` ("Eng"), and the decision sheet
`Decisions-ShipsRound2-2026-10.md` ("D" and a number), and does not redo what they settled.

The marks:

- **read**: the page, image, code or catalogue text was seen. "read (OCR)" is a text layer
  read without the page image. "tentative" is a reading at the limit of the image.
- **catalogue**: from a catalogue entry only; the document itself was not seen.
- **measured**: off an image; the image key (below) gives the image and its scale, and the
  uncertainty is stated.
- **observed**: in a run of the game's engine (scratch scripts outside the repository).
- **snippet**: a search engine's or a full-text search's excerpt only.
- **secondary**: a modern book or Wikipedia, in copyright or unchecked; a pointer only.
- **memory**: not read during this research.
- **judgement**: reasoning, by the researchers or by this note.

Winfield is not quoted and no 74 entry of his is held (§12). Quotations are from
public-domain pages only, and none runs past 30 words.

**Units and datums.** Lengths in English feet and inches, with metres in brackets. Tons are
tons burthen unless "tons of 35 cu ft" (displacement; one is 1.016 t). Steel weighs sea water
at 64⅜ lb a cubic foot, so one of his tons is 34.80 cu ft (Steel 1805 p. 206, read); "Steel
tons" says so where it matters. Positions along the ship are feet forward of **AP**, the red
dashed after perpendicular of ZAZ1357, the after end of the 168-ft length on the gun deck and
the zero of every position here. In metres they are given from the **mid-perpendicular**,
84 ft forward of AP, positive forward. Heights are above **WL1**, the top green waterline of
ZAZ1357, taken as the load line (§3.2.1), or above **K**, the lowest drawn line under the keel
(the scale strip's top border), 0.59 ft below the main keel's lower edge, as on the 64's
ZAZ1407 (Ind §2.3.6).

### 1.2 The recommendation

**The owner's choice (2026-10-28):** the *Bellerophon* is this package's 74, as recommended
below. A *Téméraire*-class French 74 is the second ship of the line, a later goal outside this
package; §5.9 and §8.4 are where her research starts.

**HMS *Bellerophon*, the Arrogant-class 74 of 1786, built on the Bellona-class draughts.**
She is the brief's ship exactly: a common ("small-class") 74 of 28 long 32-pounders on the
lower deck, 18s above and 9s on the quarterdeck and forecastle, with some 590 men. Her career
runs through the whole era of the game (1786-1815), and four of her actions lie on the chart.
Her sailing is described in print and in her own journals, and a painting of 1815 shows her
mizen. The round-1 premise that her hull would come from *Elephant*'s full-resolution sheet
was wrong (that file is the deck plans only, §3.7), but the second round found a better
basis. The Arrogant class was designed and contracted to the Bellona class's own figures
(46 ft 9 in, 1,604 tons: *Elephant*'s contract column, ZAZ1070, and Steel's *Audacious*,
p. 212), and the Bellona inboard profile ZAZ1359 records a copy sent "to Messrs Graves at
Limehouse for the Bellerophon" in 1783 (read; the year tentative). So the full-resolution Bellona sheets are her
own design's lines, profile and decks, with two recorded Arrogant alterations: the foremast
1 ft 2 in further aft, and the forecastle beams (§5.2). Her lower sills can be taken from her
sister *Elephant*'s building draught (5.6 ft, §3.1) or from the Bellona sheet (5.45 ft).

**What the alternatives gain or lose.**

- ***Bellona***: gains the same sheets under her own name, three contemporary models and the
  period's best words for any 74's sailing; loses on-chart single-ship history (her
  Copenhagen and West Indies years are off the chart; she parted company before Strachan's
  action of 1805; Basque Roads 1809 was a fleet's), and every sheet of her is of 1758-59.
- ***Elephant*, *Vanguard*, *Goliath***: gain the fullest Arrogant-class set (1280 px) and
  *Vanguard*'s knots; their famous days are off the chart.
- ***Defence***: the Bellona class with the First of June, the Nile and Trafalgar, and a
  Pocock painting (catalogue); less evidence than *Bellerophon*, and no scenario on the
  chart of her own.
- ***Mars***: gains the most complete single-ship set (1280 px), a rigged model, and the
  richest single-ship actions on or near the chart (the *Hercule* in the Raz, the *Rhin*,
  the *Infatigable*); loses the brief's ship (a 24-pounder upper deck and 634-640 men), the
  full-resolution sheets and Slade.
- ***A Téméraire-class French 74***: gains the enemy's line of battle (one file for over a
  hundred ships, secondary), and the *Droits de l'Homme* action with the razee and *Amazon*,
  both ships the game holds. Loses the brief's 32-pounders and 590 men, and every
  full-resolution hull sheet. It is sourceable in the public domain to about the British
  74's standard (§8.4). **Proposed as the next ship of the line, not this one**, and as the
  answer to D25 if the owner wants the *Droits* action (§11).

### 1.3 In brief

- **The hull is measured** at full resolution on the Bellona-class lines: the load line, the
  draughts, the half-breadths, the midship section, every port on three decks, the decks,
  the wales, the lateral plane and the rudder (§3). Two checkers re-measured it to within
  the stated uncertainties, with five corrections taken here.
- **The lower sills against the water.** The midship lower sill stands 5 ft 5½ in (1.66 m)
  above the drawn load line on the Bellona sheet and 5.6 ± 0.3 ft on *Elephant*'s building
  draught. The period's band is 5 ft 1½ in to 6 ft 0 in (§3.1). Under 70's rule (shut within a
  foot of the water) the midship lee port shuts at 11.3° of steady heel. In the engine that
  heel comes close-hauled under plain sail at about 28-30 kn of wind, or in the gusts at
  24 kn (§7.4). Every period account of a 74's lower ports shut blames swell or rolling, not
  steady heel (§6.1).
- **The rig** is Steel's 74 column (1794), whose heading's 1,799 tons is *Valiant*'s burthen,
  a larger ship: scaled 0.956-0.974 for a 168-ft 74 (§5.3). The mizen was a lateen yard in
  1794 and a gaff by 1815 (Chalon's painting of her), the change undated (§5.4).
- **The guns and people** are sourced by date: the 1793 establishment, the carronade orders
  of 1794 and 1797, the 82-gun cap at Trafalgar, the complement of 600 and then 590 by column,
  the marines' officers and NCOs (11); the private marines are judgement (about 100-110) (§4).
- **The quarter bill by deck** has period evidence for the officers by deck (an 80 of 1794 and
  Cumby's *Bellerophon* at Trafalgar), not a period bill (§4.5).
- **The engine**: a scratch 74 with her own particulars was sailed, heeled, tacked and timed
  (§7). A crewed 74 runs at 0.92-0.99 of a crewed frigate's rate; three 74s crewed should run
  about 106-147 ticks a second on the build machine against the floor of 60. The 74 alone in
  truth 51's form is estimated at 500-620 against the floor of 500, so that pin waits on the
  lead's measurement.
- **The scenario** proposed is Basque Roads, 13-15 July 1815: *Bellerophon* blockading the
  French frigates under Aix, from Maitland's narrative, with the game's 36 and brig files as
  stand-ins (§8).
- **"Three times the frigate's"**: the sources give 590 to 640 against the frigate's 264,
  2.2 to 2.4 times (§9).

- The slice between launch and load, which rests only on the traced WL1-WL3, is 1,184 tons on
  the Bellona hull against Steel's 1,267 (-7%). So the gap is not WL1 being the wrong line, nor
  the bottom below WL5: the *Audacious*, or the Navy Board's calculation that Steel copied, is
  4-9% fuller than the Bellona drawing at the same draught, at launch and at load alike
  (judgement). No full-resolution Arrogant lines exist to test it (§10).
- *Bombay Castle*'s 2,700 tons is the paper's own estimate at a mean draught 0.9 ft deeper than

  supports the measured hull, not Steel's *Audacious* (judgement).

  74 at 2,821 t gave the same polar as at 2,521 t to 0.1 kn (observed, §7.4).

**The load split** (Steel, read): the *Audacious*'s hull at launch is 54.4% of the load
displacement, furniture 4.3%, the light ship 58.7%, the real burthen 41.3%. The only itemised

### 4.4 The marines

- **Officers and NCOs**: 11 for a 74 of either column (above). The table gives no privates; their
  pay is referred to the Table of Marine Pay (read).
- **Detachments embarked in 1803** (*Naval Chronicle* vol. IX, Plymouth Report p. 245, page
  image): on 21 March a captain, three subalterns, six serjeants and 100 rank and file for the
  *Mars*, 74, fitting in Hamoaze; on 16 March a captain and 100 marines for the *Spartiate*, 74,
  and a lieutenant and 50 for the *Doris*, 44. At Portsmouth on 17 March the *Russell*, 74, had
  "her complement of marines" made up "to the war establishment" (p. 248, read (OCR)). So a
  fixed number existed, but it was not found in print. That *Mars* was then of the 640
  establishment is judgement, not sourced.
- **Shares**: Miles (1841, peace establishments, after the period; page images) gives a first
  rate 160 marines of 886 (a captain, 3 subalterns, 8 NCOs, 148 fifers, drummers and privates;
  18%) and a third rate 125 of 540 (a captain, 2 subalterns, 122 others; 23%) (pp. 44, 57; the
  OCR's 640 and 126 were misreadings). Field (1924; secondary) gives a 100-gun ship 1 captain,
  3 subalterns and 100 men in 1742 and 166 NCOs and men in 1810. At Trafalgar marines were 24 of
  *Bellerophon*'s 150 killed and wounded (16%) and 24 of *Mars*'s 98 (24%) (*Naval Chronicle*
  vol. XIV, read (OCR); weak evidence of size). The fleet's vote for 1805 was 114,000 men,
  nearly 35,000 of them marines, some in barracks (RUSI 1897 p. 1000).
- **For the file** (judgement): 1 captain, 3 lieutenants, 3 serjeants, 2 corporals, 2 drummers and
  about 100-110 privates, about a fifth of 590; marines in all 100-125. The 1803 detachment's shape
  (6 serjeants, 100 rank and file) is the alternative for the earlier fits.
- **Soldiers for marines**: early in the war troops served in lieu of marines. Detachments of the
  Queen's and the 29th served as marines in Howe's fleet in 1794 (James vol. 1 p. 155 n. 1);
  *Bellerophon*'s dead on 1 June 1794 included "one marine or soldier" (vol. 1 p. 172; Clowes
  vol. 4 p. 183); *Bellona*'s wounded at Copenhagen included a captain and five privates of foot
  (James vol. 3 p. 54) (all read (OCR)).
- **Where they stood**: "generally quartered on the poop and forecastle, or gangway, under the
  direction of their officers", sometimes at the great guns in distant cannonading (Falconer,
  QUARTERS, read (OCR)). *Bellerophon*'s marines were on her poop on 1 June 1794 under "Major
  Smith, serving as Captain of Marines", and complained of the *Caesar*'s grape coming over them;
  the witnesses were Captain Hope, Rear-Admiral Pasley and Smith himself (Molloy court martial,
  1795, read (OCR)). Her captain of marines at Trafalgar was James Wemyss (Cumby p. 725). Marines
  were not to be obliged to go aloft (Regulations p. 422, read (OCR)).
- **Small arms**: Falconer's Admiralty numbers to the small arms are 100 for a third rate of 80
  guns and 80 for one of 70 (no 74 named); the Regulations' table of seamen trained to muskets
  for service ashore gives a 74 100 (p. 142, page image). In the *Caesar* (80) on 1 June 1794 the
  small-arms men on the poop hauled braces and several were broken off to the great guns, most of
  them later replacing the killed and wounded (Molloy, read (OCR)).

### 4.5 The quarter bill evidence by deck

**No period quarter bill or station bill of a British 74 was found in print.** The only
manuscript of a 74's is RMG WQB/11, *Goliath*'s, 1801-1830, catalogue only. What there is:

- **The Regulations** (the Captain, Arts. IX-X, pp. 140-142, read): the captain quarters the
  officers and men to the guns, small arms and rigging early; a general table of stations is hung
  up, a list fixed over every gun, and the men mustered at quarters until they know them.
- **Officers by deck in a two-decker**, the *Caesar*, 80, on 29 May and 1 June 1794 (Molloy
  minutes; p. 110 read on the image, the rest (OCR)): on the quarterdeck the first lieutenant,
  the master and the captain of marines; on the upper deck the second lieutenant with the eight
  after guns and another lieutenant (the fourth, by elimination) with the eight (later seven)
  foremost; on the lower deck the third lieutenant commanding ("I commanded the lower Deck"), the
  sixth forward (the five foremost guns on 1 June) and the seventh with the five after guns; on
  the forecastle the fifth lieutenant; on the poop the lieutenant of marines, with the captain's
  clerk and a midshipman for the signals. In the *Russell*, a 74, the third lieutenant was
  quartered on the lower deck (read (OCR)).
- ***Bellerophon* at Trafalgar** (5 lieutenants, as the 1807 table gives a 74 of 590): "the fifth
  or junior lieutenant ... who commanded the seven foremost guns on each side of the lower deck"
  (Cumby p. 721, read (OCR)). Cumby, the first lieutenant, went down to the officers on the main
  and lower decks; boarders mustered under the half deck when the poop was cleared; her ports were
  hauled up close against the side when she fell aboard *L'Aigle* (pp. 723-724). She fought both
  sides at once, *Monarca* to larboard and *L'Aigle* to starboard (James vol. 3 p. 413).
- **Men to a gun** "when the ship is well manned, so as to fight both sides at once
  occasionally": 32-pdr 13, 18-pdr 9, 9-pdr 6, with often a boy to bring powder (Falconer,
  QUARTERS, read (OCR)): one crew to a gun and its opposite, as Glascock has it (1836 vol. II
  p. 234). A carronade with fixed breechings takes 4: captain, two privates and a provider
  (Douglas art. 136). The generator's rule gives 13 (14 at 58 cwt), 10, 7, 5 and 4 (§7.2).
- **Both sides and the lower deck** (Douglas 1829 arts. 150-152, pp. 178-183, page image of
  p. 181): fighting both sides, the crews of 8-pounders and under and of carronades are too few to
  divide, so only half those pieces are fought (the page reads 8-pounders, which leaves a 74's
  long 9s outside it as printed); ordered to one side, the other side's lower-deck guns are
  secured against running out by blocking the trucks, the ports shut if necessary; shifting the
  fight, the lower-deck guns are secured at the extent of their recoil, ports shut if necessary,
  and the crews cross. In the *Shannon* each gun's second captain became captain of the opposite
  piece, and boarders, sail-trimmers and firemen came from the gun crews when called (art. 120).
- **Quartering by part of ship** (Glascock 1836 vol. II pp. 231-234, after the period, read (OCR)):
  forecastlemen and fore-topmen forward; main- and mizen-topmen and afterguard aft; carpenters by
  the pumps, a shipwright by the rudder head; each gun and its opposite drawn equally from both
  watches.
- **A post-period analogue** (US *Ordnance Instructions* 1860, App. A, page images; for proportion
  only): an 84-gun two-decker's skeleton watch bill of 721 excluding officers and marines
  (forecastlemen 88, foretopmen 76, maintopmen 84, mizzentopmen 60, afterguard 166, waisters 139,
  and others); lower deck 16 guns a side at 14 men and a boy, main deck 12-14 and a boy, spar deck
  8-12 and a boy; a lieutenant, a midshipman and a quarter gunner to each division.

**The arithmetic of one broadside** (judgement; checked): manning one side of the 1795 fit takes
14 lower-deck crews of 13, 14 upper-deck of 9, 7 quarterdeck 9-pdrs of 6, two forecastle 9s of 6,
a forecastle carronade and 3 poop carronades of 4: 378 men by Falconer and Douglas, about 402 by
the generator. Of 590 that leaves about 190-210 for 14 officers, 19 mates and midshipmen, the
marines (100-125, of whom the small arms take about 80-100), about 34 at the powder (the gunner's
mates, yeomen and the 30 boys), the helm, the carpenters at the pumps and the cockpit. So the
trimmers come from the gun crews when sail work calls for them, as in a frigate (Douglas art. 120).

**A judgement split of 590 at quarters** for the design: lower deck about 196 (14 crews of 13 and a
boy), upper deck about 140, quarterdeck and forecastle about 63, at the guns about 400; small arms
about 90, mostly the marines on the poop, quarterdeck and gangways; about 100 others. Officers by
deck, after the *Caesar* and Cumby: the captain, first lieutenant, master and captain of marines
on the quarterdeck; two lieutenants on the upper deck (aft and forward); two on the lower deck,
the junior with the seven foremost guns a side (that one sourced); the forecastle under a master's
mate or the boatswain, a 74 of five lieutenants having none to spare; the marine subalterns, a
signal midshipman and the clerk on the poop. The split of the watch bill (forecastle, tops,
afterguard, waisters) for 590 is unsourced for the period (§10).

## 5. The rig and the sails

### 5.1 What Steel's 74 is

- Steel 1794 vol. I p. 49 heads the column "74 GUNS. 1799 TONS." (both IA copies; the `_1_0` copy
  is the clearer). The diameters are "Taken at the Partners", which in two-deckers are at the
  upper deck (read).
- **1,799 tons is exactly *Valiant*'s burthen** (1759; 171 ft 2¼ in x 49 ft 4 in; Charnock
  p. 247, page image). His 80 column's 1,615 tons is exactly *Cambridge*'s (1755; 166-0 x 47-0;
  Charnock p. 246, page image), and his 64 column's 1,369 tons is Folio I's 64. His worked example
  on p. 39 is a 74 of 176 ft x 48 ft 8 in, main mast 112 ft 4 in by his rule, and Folio I's 74 has
  those dimensions at 1,828 tons (read). So which ship the 74 column was drawn for is open, a
  *Valiant*-size 74 or the 176-ft example; either way it is larger than a 168-ft Bellona or
  Arrogant (checker; the first round's "a 176-ft ship" was overstated).
- **Steel's rule** (p. 39, read): the main mast is half the sum of the lower-deck length and the
  extreme breadth. On the Arrogant class's design figures (168 ft + 46 ft 9 in) it gives 107 ft
  4½ in, 0.967 of the column's 111 ft 0 in. The table sits 1.2% under the rule on the 176-ft
  example and 0.7% over it on *Valiant*. **A Bellona-size main mast is therefore 106 ft 1 in to
  108 ft 1 in, the spars scaled 0.956-0.974 and the sail areas 0.914-0.949** (judgement; on
  *Bellona*'s 46 ft 11 in the rule gives 107 ft 5½ in, on Charnock's 168-3 x 47-4 107 ft 9½ in).
  Steel's own table departs from his rule, so the band is the honest figure.
- **No period mast list** for *Bellona*, *Bellerophon* or their classes was found; ZAZ1359 and
  ZAZ1358 carry no mast and yard table (read). RMG ADT0253, a rigging warrant for *Monarch*
  (1765), is catalogue only.

## 6. How she sailed: truth candidates

Each gives the date, place, wind, sail, behaviour and source. Public-domain print can be pinned
as written; speeds marked "computed" are this note's arithmetic. A truth pins states, counts and
bands, never digests (the brief). The candidates drawn from engine runs are marked "(engine)" and
measured in §7.

### 6.1 The lee lower ports

**What the period says.** No public-domain source gives a heel in degrees at which a 74's lower
ports were shut. Every account of a 74's lower ports shut names swell, rolling or a high sea:

1. ***Droits de l'Homme*, French 74, 13-14 January 1797**, a hard westerly, thick, a rising sea:
   Pellew made her out as "two tier of guns, with her lower-deck ports shut, and that she had no
   poop" (his letter, *Naval Chronicle* vol. VIII p. 466, read (OCR)). Her lower-deck ports, "being
   nearer to the water's edge by 14 inches than those of the generality of French 74s, were obliged
   to be shut almost as soon as opened"; James blames the sea and the heavy rolling for want of
   sail to steady her (vol. 2 p. 14, read (OCR)). Pipon, a prisoner in her: opened at the start
   and soon shut again for the great sea, the water running on the cables in the cable tier (NC
   vol. VIII p. 465). Troude: on her new point of sailing, "a little more pressed", Lacrosse tried
   to open the lower battery and "il fallut y renoncer : la mer entrait à pleins sabords" (t. III
   pp. 55-57, read (OCR)).
2. ***Blenheim*, 74, 6 August 1805**, in 19°9' S (the Indian Ocean, off the chart): "Owing,
   however, to the great swell that prevailed, the Blenheim could not open her lower-deck ports";
   she fought *Marengo* and *Belle-Poule* with her 18-pdrs, a few 9s and carronades (James vol. 4
   p. 51, read (OCR)). That she was a cut-down 90 is memory, not James.
3. ***Monarch*, 74, 25 September 1806**, 6-7 leagues off Chasseron (on the chart), wind N by E,
   chasing SSW: the heavy swell kept her lower-deck ports shut for much of her action with three
   French frigates, and in about 20 minutes she was much disabled (vol. 4 pp. 175-176, read (OCR)).
4. ***Queen Charlotte*, 100, 29 May 1794**: her lower deck full of water from the lowness of her
   ports (4 ft 6 in) and the roughness of the sea, the pumps going much of the night (vol. 1
   p. 154, page image).
5. ***Brunswick*, 74, 1 June 1794**: locked with the *Vengeur*, her men blew off eight lower-deck
   starboard ports they could not open; afterwards she secured her lower-deck ports, the water
   rushing in at every roll (vol. 1, OCR l. 14389-14390, and p. 181; read (OCR)). ***Illustrious***, 74, hove to in a
   violent SE gale on 17-18 March 1795, shipped much water through lower-deck port lids damaged in
   action (p. 293).
6. ***Jupiter*, 50, 1799**: opened her lower-deck ports against the *Preneuse*, shut them again and
   fought with her 12-pdrs, for the turbulent sea and the little height allowable to such small
   two-deckers (vol. 2 p. 393, read (OCR)).
7. **Three armed Indiamen, 3 July 1810**, close-hauled in an increasing breeze, the third reef
   taken in the topsails: "even then the ships lay over so much, and the sea ran so high, that they
   could not keep open their lower-deck lee ports" (vol. 5 p. 133, read (OCR)). The cleanest
   statement of heel and sea at a stated sail; not 74s.
8. **The rule of the period**: of the weather line, "If the sea is rough, and the wind boisterous,
   it cannot readily fight with the lower-deck battery"; larger ships use the lower tier more safely
   in a high sea (Falconer 1780, LINE and BOTTOM, read (OCR)). When it blows fresh, weather ships
   seldom have their lower-deck guns elevated enough, the inclined guns run out again after firing,
   and often the lower tier cannot be used at all, while the lee fleet works its own (Steel 1794
   vol. II, Naval Tactics, read (OCR)). A three-decker that can open her lower ports on a wind only
   in smooth water may be taken by a properly built 74, and a 74 lacking stability "may be as easily
   taken by a thirty-eight-gun frigate" (Steel 1805 p. 124, page image). The Regulations provide for
   ships of the line whose lower-deck ports bad weather keeps shut (pp. 159, 276, read (OCR)). British
   ships of the 1740s were complained of as heeling so much in blowing weather that they could not
   open their lee ports (Fincham 1851 p. 81, read (OCR)).
9. **The heeling trials** (*Collection* p. 11; Steel p. 140; page images): fourteen lower-deck guns
   run out on one side heeled *Bombay Castle* 3 in at the side (about 0.6°), and 8 in with the men at
   quarters (about 1.6°); the crank French *Scipio* (74) 13 in and 24 in, so crank that her lower-deck
   guns were difficult and dangerous to use in fine weather, until her sides were doubled with a foot
   of light wood (Steel p. 139 after Romme; *Collection* pp. 9-10). The engine has no heel from weights
   run out (`physics/hull.py:352-367`), so these are not pinnable (§7.3).

**The candidates:**

- **P1, the sill (a static band).** The 74's midship lower-deck sill stands 1.55-1.83 m (5 ft 1½ in
  to 6 ft 0 in) above her load line (§3.1); the file carries 1.66 m (ZAZ1357) or 1.71 m (ZAZ1064)
  as a sourced figure in its hull profile.
- **P2, the state on the function** (engine). On the file's sill and half-breadth, `lee_port_shut`
  has the midship lee port open half a degree below the threshold and shut half a degree above it:
  for 1.66 m on 6.92 m, open at 10.5° and shut at 11.5° (the threshold 11.29°; 10.92° on half the
  beam). Pin the sill and the two states, not the angle's digits. On the generator's convention
  (§7.2) the same port is already shut at 10.5°, so the pin also proves the file carries the
  sourced sill.
- **P3, in ordinary sailing** (engine; truth 10's world, no book, plain sail, close-hauled): the
  lee lower ports are open at 24 kn of wind and shut at 32 kn, at either displacement (2,521 t:
  8.2° and 14.8°; 2,821 t: 7.3° and 13.2°), with at least 1.9° of margin. At 30 kn the margin is only
  0.3° at 2,821 t, so 30 is not pinned.
- **P4, the battery she keeps**: at that heel the fourteen lee lower guns are silent while the upper
  deck (shut only near 28-30°), the quarterdeck and the weather lower tier fire. Pin the count of
  silent guns.
- **P5, the frigate unmoved**: the frigate-36's lee ports stay open at the 74's shut heel (they shut
  at 21.0°).
- **P6, nothing existing moves** (engine): the most heel while firing in the first action (the
  cutter 2.7°, the *Renard* 6.2°) and on the gate's whole day (the frigate 4.0°, the 74 3.3°) is far
  below the existing ships' shut heels (21-25°), so wiring the rule moves none of today's combat
  pins on heel alone. Re-measure 86's privateer schooner (about 11°) when it is in.
- **P7, the sea** (only if roll is wired, §11): the *Droits de l'Homme*'s case, a low tier rolling
  heavily in a hard westerly, is not steady heel. A rule on heel alone cannot reproduce items 1-3
  above, and the truth should say so beside it.

### 6.2 Her sailing, by the accounts

- **S1, *Bellerophon*'s trim** (qualitative): on 28 May 1794, a fresh squally southerly,
  close-hauled on the starboard tack, she led the flying squadron and was told to shorten sail,
  but "from some defect in her trim" was the slowest sailer of her division (James vol. 1
  pp. 145-146, read (OCR)); the NRS editor calls her flagship of the fast squadron (vol. 1 p. 71).
  On 16-17 June 1795, the wind WNW and then northerly, she and *Brunswick*, "the former in
  particular, had always been considered as excellent sailers" but were "quite out of trim" from
  their stowage; though they cut away anchors and launches, started water and provisions, and she
  threw over four poop carronades and much shot, they still held the squadron back (vol. 1
  pp. 265-266, read (OCR)). On 19 October 1805 she was among the five ships that "showed their
  superiority of sailing" (Walker's letter, NRS vol. 2 p. 323; James vol. 3 p. 387). A file note:
  the engine keeps no weight inventory, so trim is not a truth unless it is modelled.
- **S2, *Bellona*'s handiness**: on 9 June 1794, a light northerly, with *Ganges* and *Alexander*
  sailing ill, she kept company for most of the chase "had her topsails on the cap, her courses
  hauled up, and her yards braced by" (James vol. 1 p. 190); on 5 January 1795, light airs and
  squally, "one of the fastest and handiest 74s in the British navy" (p. 332) (read (OCR)). If the
  game ever holds a dull 74, pin that her best speed exceeds it by a clear margin.
- **S3, missing stays in a seaway**: *Defence* (Bellona class), 29 May 1794, fresh breezes and a
  great westerly swell after the third reef in the topsails, missed stays at about 4.45 p.m. and
  wore, falling to leeward of the line (NRS vol. 1 pp. 106-113; the editor warns that Consett's
  copy has inaccuracies). A probability band, not a certainty.
- **S4, a sail-state sequence**: *Goliath*, 14 February 1797 off St Vincent: out second reef of the
  topsails at 2.30 a.m., courses set at 3, out first reef of the fore topsail at 9, light breezes
  and hazy; tacked by signal at 2 p.m. (NRS vol. 1 pp. 225-227, read (OCR)).
- **S5, light airs**: Trafalgar, 21 October 1805: *Bellerophon* at 4 a.m. in light airs inclinable
  to calm with a heavy westerly swell, out all reefs at daylight, studding sails at noon (her
  journals, NRS vol. 2 pp. 272-274); the fleet scarcely 3 kn under studding sails (James vol. 3
  p. 391), *Victory*'s log 2½ kn to noon (the editor). Band: under 3 kn.
- **S6, ready to slip**: *Bellerophon* in Basque Roads, 13 July 1815, at anchor with slip buoys on
  her cables, "the top-sail and top-gallant yards were swayed to the mast-heads, the sails stopt
  with rope yarns, and every thing kept ready to make sail at a moment's warning" (Maitland
  pp. 39-40, read (OCR)). About 11 July she beat out of the Pertuis d'Antioche against a flood tide
  just made (p. 37); on 26 July she beat up from the Start to Plymouth Sound against a strong
  northerly in a forenoon (pp. 123-124). An evolution truth: the time to get under way from that
  state.

### 6.3 Speeds

- **S7, a polar point** (an Arrogant, *Vanguard*, 1 August 1798, NRS vol. 2 p. 53, page image; the
  checker's reading of the columns): moderate breezes and clear; "Set driver. ½ past 1, set
  mainsail." Hours 1-2, 4 kn 6 f and 5 kn 2 f on SE with the wind NNW (14 points off); hour 3,
  2 kn 4 f hauled to the wind while unbending the cable (a manoeuvre, not a polar point); hour 4,
  5 kn on E by N with the wind N (7 points off); hour 5, 5 kn 4 f on E by S (9 points off, a point
  abaft the beam). The editor: "if the ship was going five and a half knots with the wind one point
  abaft the beam, she certainly did not make more than six knots" (pp. 6-7). Band: 4.75-5.5 kn ±1 kn
  in a moderate breeze (Beaufort 4, judgement) under courses, topsails and driver; the scratch 74
  makes 5.2 kn at 100° in 10 kn of wind (engine, §7.4).
- **S8, reduced sail, a lower bound**: 1 June 1794, a fresh breeze at S by W, Howe's fleet steering
  about NW (11 points off) under single-reefed topsails (the night's order was foresail, single-
  reefed topsails, jib and main topmast staysail: NRS vol. 1 p. 75), "going at the rate of very
  little more than five knots an hour" (James vol. 1 p. 163, read (OCR)). A fleet keeping station,
  so a lower bound on a 74 under that sail (checker).
- **S9, running in battle**: San Domingo, 6 February 1806: the engaged 74s ran nearly before the
  wind at about 8 kn, *Superb* having shortened sail; the breeze light at the start, its strength
  after the shift not given (James vol. 4 pp. 95-96, read (OCR)).
- **S10, before a strong wind**: off Toulon, 13 February 1814, a strong easterly: Cosmao's squadron,
  the 74 *Romulus* sternmost, ran under all sail before the wind at 10 kn, the 98 *Boyne* pressing
  sail and closing her (James vol. 6 pp. 117-118, read (OCR)). A squadron's pace, set by its
  slowest. On 12 July 1801 a Franco-Spanish squadron in the Straits made 9-10 kn in a freshened
  breeze (Troude t. III, read (OCR)).
- **S11, a French 74's best**: the *Droits de l'Homme* at 11 kn running ESE before a fresh westerly
  when the frigates were signalled; after losing her fore and main topmasts, 5 kn under the two
  courses and the mizen topsail (Troude t. III p. 56; James vol. 2 p. 13; *Victoires* p. 294); James
  judges the frigates "probably running a third faster".
- **S12, scudding**: the *Hero*, 74, and the brig *Grasshopper*, 23-24 December 1811, a hard gale,
  running SW then SSW: the brig, keeping station on her commodore, made 9-10 kn under a close-reefed
  main topsail (Brenton vol. 2 p. 390, read (OCR)). The *Hero*'s sail is not stated; her speed was at
  least the brig's.
- **S13, a passage**: Nelson's fleet of 74s with *Victory*, Cape St Vincent to Barbados, 12 May to
  4 June 1805, 3,227 miles: about 140 miles a day, 5.8 kn; home 3,459 miles at 3.9-4.2 kn (Nelson's
  own 93-mile average and James's dates disagree) (James vol. 3 pp. 346, 353, read (OCR); computed).
- **S14, an upper band** (an analogue, the 120 *Caledonia*'s report: James vol. 4 p. 483, read
  (OCR)): close-hauled under whole or single-reefed topsails 9 kn; double-reefed 7; under courses 4;
  large under all sail 11 kn 6 f; before the wind 9 kn 4 f; carries her helm half a turn a-weather;
  stays, wears and lies-to well. No 74's own report was found in print (§10).
- **The best speed** (judgement): no public-domain source gives a 74's best as the frigate-36's file
  has Winfield's 12-13 kn for 18-pounder frigates. The figures found (10 kn before a strong wind,
  a squadron's; 11 kn for a French 74; 8 kn in battle) suggest a best speed of 10-11 kn, below the
  frigate's (§11).

### 6.4 Relative speeds

- **S15, the *Duquesne* chase, 24-25 July 1803** (off the chart, the West Indies; London Gazette
  15642 p. 1553, page image; Troude t. III pp. 291-294, page images of pp. 292-293; James vol. 3
  pp. 185-187; Marshall, Walker): the two French 74s slipped out of Cap-François at dusk in a heavy
  squall that soon moderated, then light land winds. *Vanguard*, to leeward in the squall, joined at
  midnight and with the *Tartar* was headmost by noon, ahead of her sister *Bellerophon*
  (Commodore Loring), and took the *Duquesne* 15 or 18 miles east of Cape Maisi at about 3.30-4 p.m.
  The *Duquesne*, with 275 men, 32 of them sick, and 12 lower-deck guns manned, was taken aback
  while luffing "with all her upper sails and studding sails out" (Troude). The *Duguay-Trouin*,

### 7.2 The two-decker in the generator

- **No gun has a deck.** `GUN_KEYS` are id, kind, pounder, place, number, side, x_m, height_m,
  crew and weight_cwt (`freesail/ship/schema.py:194-205`), and a fit refuses two broadside guns with
  one (number, side) (1442-1448). So a two-decker's tiers must be numbered on from deck to deck
  (1-14 lower, 15-28 upper, and so on) and are told apart only by `height_m` (read). The period
  numbered each deck from No. 1 (the Regulations, the Gunner), so the file departs from it and
  should say so.
- **`height_m` does double duty.** `GunSpec` calls it the muzzle's height above the water upright
  (`schema.py:506-525`); the generator's `PORT_SILL_M` 0.6 m is "the port sill above the deck, for a
  gun's muzzle height" (`tools/gen_ships.py:3451-3454`); `shot.py:22-24` reads the sill at the gun's
  height. The brief's hull profile carries sourced sills per tier, and the lee-port wiring should
  read the sill from it (§7.3). By the generator's convention the 74's lower sill would be her lower
  deck (0.98 m above WL1, the middle red line) plus 0.6 m, 1.58 m against the measured 1.66 m. The
  64's happened to agree (0.93 + 0.6 = 1.53 m, her port 7) (judgement).
- **`deck_height_m`** is the frigate's uppermost continuous deck (`gen_ships.py:1475`;
  `frigate-36.yaml:34-35`), the 74's upper deck at 3.2-3.3 m (§3.3). Guns are placed at the deck
  plus `PORT_SILL_M`, so the lower tier would need a negative offset that no builder writes today
  (`gen_ships.py:3454, 3572-3573`). It drives the deck's apparent wind (`physics/hull.py:388`), the
  sails' deck wind and spar windage heights (`physics/sails.py:349, 1562`), the height of eye
  (`world/lookout.py:215`), the readings' apparent wind (`api/readings.py:382`) and the side the
  viewer draws (`api/queries.py:396`); the schema defaults it to 1.5. Sail centre heights are file
  figures, so it does not move the sails' heeling moment. D18 is open "with the owner's measure";
  with the hull profile drawn (D14(c)) it can serve the physics and the spar heights alone.
- **The quarter bill has no deck.** `QuarterBillSpec` is trimmers, helm, powder and priority
  (`schema.py:208-212, 471-484, 1449-1457`; `gen_ships.py:785-802`); the crews come from the fit's
  per-gun figures. The runtime draws each crew's stations by the gun's x: forward when x is at least
  a sixth of the length from midships, aft when x is a sixth or more abaft, the waist between, so
  the ends are thirds of the hull (`freesail/crew/quarters.py:398-417`); a carronade's crew draws
  marines first; robbing goes by x (`_rob_order`); "fire by divisions" splits at x >= 0
  (`combat/guns.py:476`); `Gun.pair_key` is "gun:N" (`ship/parts.py:533-538`); `guns_named` knows
  "No. N", bow, stern and swivels, and no "lower deck" (`orders/gunnery.py:510-550`). So lower- and
  upper-deck guns at one x are stationed, robbed and fired together (read). A quarter bill by deck
  needs a deck key on the gun, which the brief allows "where a new figure needs a key", or numbering
  and notes only (§11).
- **The marines' division** gets whatever the gun crews leave. With a 74's crews (about 402 men to
  41 pairs at 590) it is empty; with the frigate's 40 guns at 590 men, 67 marines reach it (observed,
  checker). `lend()` frees no marine: it only names them in the log line ("their fire is 73's",
  `quarters.py:292-303`). A 74's captain and subalterns of marines are new posts in her people file
  (the frigate's 12 posts have none), and the detachment and kit are 73's.
- **An all-hands evolution at quarters** under "sail before guns" always takes `TOLD_OFF_SHARE`,
  half of every gun crew, even when the trimmers suffice (`evolutions/runner.py:1129-1140`;
  `quarters.py:70`).
- **The rules of thumb**: `gun_crew` (`gen_ships.py:3430-3436`) gives 13 for a 55-cwt 32-pdr, 14 at
  58 cwt, 10 for an 18-pdr of 40-42 cwt, 7 for a 9-pdr of 24½ cwt, 5 for a 32-pdr carronade and 4
  for an 18-pdr carronade, against Falconer's and Douglas's 13, 9, 6, 4 and 4. `LONG_GUN_CWT` has no
  24- or 32-pounder (3416-3417); the tables are only indexed, so adding keys is byte-safe.

### 8.3 The candidates

**(a) Basque Roads, 13-15 July 1815, *Bellerophon* watching the frigates (recommended).**

- **The history** (Maitland 1826, read (OCR); pages from the checkers):
  - 31 May: under Île d'Aix "two large frigates, a ship corvette, and a large brig, all ready for
    sea": the *Méduse*, *Saale*, *Bayadère* and *Épervier* (p. 3). He sent the *Myrmidon* off
    Bordeaux and the *Cephalus* to Arcachon (p. 8).
  - 11-12 July: the *Myrmidon* off the Maumusson, the *Slaney* rejoined, the *Cyrus* off the Baleine
    light; the *Bellerophon* and *Slaney* ran into Basque Roads, beating out of the Pertuis
    d'Antioche against a flood tide just made (pp. 37-38).
  - 13 July: the frigates about three miles off, ready for sea, topgallant yards across and
    studding-sail gear rove; the *Bellerophon* at anchor ready to slip (§6.2, S6) (pp. 39-40).
  - 15 July: at daybreak the *Épervier* stood out under a flag of truce; by 5.30 the ebb had failed
    with the wind "blowing right in"; the *Superb* in the offing (pp. 68-69).
  - Bonaparte asked whether two frigates with 24-pounders on their main decks were a match for a 74;
    Maitland was told one at Aix had changed her 18s for 24s (p. 77 n., hearsay). He "scarcely
    considered three frigates a match for one line-of-battle ship"; to break out, the frigates would
    have had to beat 3-4 leagues out through a narrow passage under a 74 to windward (pp. 77-78).
- **On the chart**: biscay-north; Île d'Aix lies at about 46.0° N 1.2° W (memory), near the block's
  southern edge. Whether the chart has the Pertuis and Aix road in harbour detail was not checked.
- **The ships the game can field** (checker): the *Méduse* (Pallas class, a French 40: 28 x 18-pdr,
  8 x 8, carronades, 326 men; secondary) and the *Saale* (Armide class; secondary) only as the
  frigate-36 under French colours (a British 36 of 26 x 18 and 264 men), as D25(c) uses her for the
  *Virginie*. James calls the *Méduse* a French 40-gun frigate (vol. 6 p. 147), whose class he gives
  44 guns and 330 men (vol. 1 p. 59). The *Épervier* (a 16-gun brig, by search, not reopened): the brig
  file, the Diligence-class *Harpy* (316 tons, 16 x 32-pdr carronades and 2 x 6, 121 men), is the
  nearest. The *Cephalus*, an 18-gun brig-sloop: the brig file's guns and men on a smaller hull. The
  *Bayadère* (a ship-corvette, rate not found) and the 20-gun ship-sloops *Slaney*, *Myrmidon* and
  *Cyrus* (which went to sea with 18 x 32-pdr carronades and 2 long 9s: James vol. 5 pp. 434-435) have
  no file. Troude's Rochefort division (the *Saal*, *Circé* and *Bayadère* at Aix) is of May 1812 and
  1814, not 1815 (t. IV pp. 153, 182; checker).
- **What it needs**: the 1815 fit (the gaff, sourced; carronades with long 9s at the shrouds); a
  French role that waits to slip out rather than engaging (a King's frigate today engages anything,
  §7.2; France's `slips_out_when_blown_off` knob is read, its `keeps_port`, `engages` and
  `keeps_the_sea` are not); pinned weather with the wind blowing into the roads, and the tides if
  the chart carries them.
- **For it**: her own action of her best-known year, on the chart, a single ship against frigates,
  with a printed narrative of positions, states and winds, and the question Bonaparte himself put.
  **Against it**: stand-ins for every French ship; no consorts' files; it was a watch, not a fight;
  and the lee-port truth is not exercised at anchor or in the roads.
