The Sherbourne is saved, hove to off St Anthony's Head in seven fathoms. I've released the watcher station, and the journal has the full notes.

What made this run good: without preset orders it felt like real seamanship. Your cross-bearing fix at 15:12 put her six miles west of the reckoning, the same westward set the frigate had. Hauling to NNE then carried her outside the Manacles. The weather was the best yet: mares' tails in the morning, an oily sky by afternoon, and half-hourly squalls veering and blowing 28–35 knots. Tacking off St Anthony's Head in the dark was a proper escape.

My mistake: the helm reading gives no side, and I read it as weather helm all day. At 17:06 it was lee helm, so my advice to keep the jib was wrong, and some of the morning's helm calls may have been too. Showing the side (weather or lee) in the reading would fix that for any agent.

The most important things for the next version:

There were no lights at night. The Lizard and St Anthony's lights were reported in daylight but not after dark. From 17:26 nothing was in sight until land was a mile ahead, which makes a night approach impossible.
The lead stopped at dark. Standing orders conditioned on "land in sight" stop just when they matter most. Your timed lead order fixed it.
Emergency orders. The tack waited in the queue behind the lead, "avast that" then cancelled the tack instead of the lead, and the tack was refused until she was close-hauled. In danger, helm orders should go first.
Hands. Trim orders brace the furled square yards first. That starves the mainsail and headsail trim, and once it cost the lead.
Reefs add up. A reef standing order fires again on every squall, which took the main to four reefs. A "to N reefs" form or a ceiling would stop the stacking.
"Shoal Water" hove her to just after the escape, where she drifted back toward the Head. The order is good in open water and bad in an entrance.

The smaller items, such as dangers that were never sighted and the log over-reading by about 15%, are in the journal.

Thank you for the voyage. I'm looking forward to the officer-of-the-watch station.
