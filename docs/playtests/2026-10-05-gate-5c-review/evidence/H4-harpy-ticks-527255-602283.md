### The nudge and the pause

- LOG 533,399 (09:09): "The officer of the watch nudged: 3 contrary orders on the yards and the helm within the watch (heave to; fill away; shape a course for plymouth)."
- LOG 533,573 (09:12): "The officer of the watch is paused: 4 contrary orders on the yards and the helm within the watch (heave to; fill away; shape a course for plymouth; steer 073) after a nudge. Continue, stand down, or leave paused? Say 'resume the officer of the watch' or 'stand down the officer of the watch'."

The four orders and why each was given:

1. `heave to` (530,110, 08:15): to put the Falmouth pilot into his cutter, in answer to the captain's own standing order, with the captain's assent 41 ticks later ("Alright by me").
2. `fill away` (533,346, 09:09): the pilot had left 6 ticks before, and the game had just told him to: "She is hove to; fill away before giving her a course."
3. `shape a course for plymouth` (533,399): two ticks after "Filled away; braced full and steering S by W (190°)", the opposite of where she was bound. The captain had re-allowed this very order 46 ticks earlier.
4. `steer 073` (533,573): a 7° correction of the shaped 66°. OFFICER: "that line runs straight onto Rame Head, so I'm steering 073° (ENE ½ E) to pass about two miles south of it".

None is contrary to the one before in a sailor's sense. Heaving to and filling away are the two ends of one evolution; a course after filling and a small correction of it are steps forward. The second order was prescribed by the game's own refusal and the third pre-approved by the captain.

How it was cleared and what it cost:

- CAPTAIN "Resume the officer of the watch" at 533,641, 68 ticks later.
- The pause did not stop the officer's orders. `set plain sail` was accepted 18 ticks into it (533,591), and `set the studdingsails` 36 ticks into it was rejected by the ship, not by the pause.
- Direct cost: one typed order and a journal entry ("That was a planned sequence to put the pilot off and bear up, not a loop. Same false positive as before (finding 12)").
- Indirect cost, arguable: after it the officer stood by a glass without retrying the studdingsails or setting the royals, and the captain asked for them half an hour later.
- By primer 16 an unanswered pause stands the station down after ten real minutes. A captain away from the keyboard would have lost his officer for dropping a pilot correctly.
- This is the only `agent.paused` in the eight-day game.

**Second nudge.** 536,096 (09:54): "nudged: 3 contrary orders on the larboard fore lower studdingsail... and 4 more within the watch (set the studdingsails; run out the stuns'ls; set the studdingsails)". The second `set` was the larboard side once its booms were out. OFFICER's journal: "The detector should compare what each order actually changed, not just its verb."

- 568,741, "Let's bring in the stuns'ls and the booms after", was answered at 573,847, 85 minutes later, with "Aye, sir: in stuns'ls, then rig in the booms" and an order for sails that had been in since 19:04.

The likelier reading is that each stand-by was already on its way when the word landed: the spacing is one tick six times out of seven, and the tobacco question was answered at the officer's very next turn. The other reading, that he read the word and stood by regardless, cannot be ruled out from the save (section 10).

### Handover notes

- 527,255, short, written for itself: plan, book, what it watched for.
- 602,100, written for a stranger, and good. Salient parts:
  - "THE GLASS IS FALLING: 30.08, down a tenth since the middle watch, after 30.44 yesterday morning. Expect wind, probably from the SW, which puts this berth on a lee-ish coast. Get into shelter while it's quiet."
  - "GRANTS for the watch: weigh, get under way, unmoor, let go the anchor, heave short, set plain sail, steer, shift the course, tack, wear, box haul, heave to, fill away, take a bearing of, work up the reckoning, observe the sun, sell, buy, send the boat, shape a course for Plymouth."
  - "HABITS THAT HELP: orders sent while you stand by are not run, so open your turn with say first. "Trim sails" acts on the wind as it is when given, so trim again after the helm has swung. The contrary-orders detector misreads planned sequences (heave to, fill away, shape a course), so say what you're doing. "Taken aback" fires urgent in calms and at anchor; it's harmless. Standing order names can't contain an apostrophe. The reckoning keeps adding distance at anchor; cross-bearings fix it".
  - One false statement: "The flood is making now". She rode to the ebb until 610,320 (06:32).

### Signs of relay and turn limits

- OFFICER's journal, 575,574: "calls held past 60 seconds time out at the device (the say calls still landed)". The journal's "the device" and the notes' "a cloud session" suggest a relay between the model and the bridge; the save does not say.
- Two spoken lines were sent twice in near-identical words: 538,638 and 538,719 ("The trial pays, sir..."), 542,505 and 542,581 ("The wind is dying, sir..."). The first copy had landed each time.
- Three calls were made out of turn and are recorded as door events, not calls: `read_log(since_tick=0, severity='notable')` at 538,703, a `shelve` at 538,707, `library(find='noon sight')` at 542,928. Each came after a `say` had handed the turn back while the model still had work to do.

- The model ended a stand-by by speaking before acting three times (527,255, 573,847, 602,084). That is the habit his note describes.

### Other tool use

`readings` five times, `read_log` three times, two of them with `since_tick=0` (the tool returns only the last 200 lines, so these are safe), `state` never. No stand-by was refused and no sign of a context or turn-budget limit appears in this slice.

## 6. Ship, sea, navigation and port observations

**1. The night off Penlee: the wind.**

- `weather.hour` carries sky, glass and sea but no wind. Strength comes from `wind.gust` means and the officer's reports.
- OFFICER's reports: WSW 10 knots (07:27), W by S 8 to 9 (09:13), SW by W 5 (11:41), W 6 (13:04), WSW (15:16), "light airs from SW" (16:18), "2 knots of air" (18:23), "W, 2 knots" (18:58), "WNW 3 knots" (20:24), "light NW by W air" (20:52), "WNW 1 knot" (04:14).
- Gust lines agree: means of 4 to 5 knots from 16:38 to 17:48, 2 to 3 from 18:42 to 19:02, 3 to 5 from 19:28 to 21:25, 2 by midnight, 1 to 2 by 03:37.
- All 8 `wind.shift` lines of the slice: 12:45 W; 15:09 WSW; 15:51 SW; 16:23 SSW; 16:24 SW; 17:02 WSW; 18:25 W; 20:18 WNW. Nothing after 20:18.
- The only flicker of the old instant-wind line is the pair at 559,422 and 559,451, 29 seconds apart.
- So the true wind was a dying air working slowly from SW round to WNW. The CHANGES file's "swung between SSW and E within minutes at six to eleven knots" does not describe this night; it fits 12:00 to 13:50 on 19 June, where the 265 lines are (for example 13:39 "Wind backed to E by N, a light breeze" and 13:50 "Wind veered to SSW, a gentle breeze").

- Seven at anchor: 20:32, 20:37, 20:44, 20:46, 20:52, 21:02, 21:12. The last three came with every square sail furled; the last two with only the spanker set, on a ship held by her anchor.

**3. A coincidence worth a look in the code.** Five of the seven at-anchor urgent lines come exactly 9 seconds after a hand-lead sounding: 574,339 and 574,348; 575,034 and 575,043; 575,539 and 575,548; 576,139 and 576,148; 576,739 and 576,748. In three other cases in this slice the urgent line follows the first per-sail backing by 9 seconds (562,451, 566,900, 574,655). If the link is causal, the officer's own "fog lead", firing every ten minutes, was timing the alarm.

**4. Anchoring.**

- Who ordered it: the officer (573,882, "By the officer of the watch: letting go the anchor"), on the leave given on 14 June. He had proposed it at 18:23 and again at 20:24 ("If you give me the deck again, I'll do it"); the captain's answer was to give the deck.
- Why there, in the OFFICER's words at 20:24: "By account we're 50°16' N, 4°15' W, which puts Rame Head about NNE under three miles. Anything north of NE by E leads straight at it, in fog, at nightfall, with no pilot... The wind is WNW 3 knots and off the land, so it's a safe berth tonight." At 18:23 he had added the coming ebb and "a night drifting blind near the Deeps and the Eddystone".
- `let go the anchor` let go with courses, topsails, topgallants, staysails, jib and spanker all set. The game raised no objection and took no sail in.
- By primer 13 this is the emergency order ("lets it go where she is, whatever her way"); `come to an anchor` is the evolution that takes sail in and ends in "Brought up by...". The officer held leave for the first and not the second (that came on 20 June at 687,262).
- The cable veered was 152 fathoms, five times the depth, where the officer had twice said "about 90 fathoms". A measured form exists (`let go the best bower in twenty fathoms`), but there "in N fathoms" means scope, while in `come to an anchor in twelve fathoms` it means depth.
- With no "brought up" line, neither the captain nor the officer could tell from the log that she was riding.

**5. Tide against stream.**

- The pilot's words (578,880): "the flood will serve from about a quarter past three in the middle watch".
- The ship swung "to the ebb" at 00:20 and "to the flood" at 06:32 (610,320, just after the slice), three and a quarter hours after the time the pilot gave.
- This is the game's own offshore stream rule (primer 13: the stream runs east from three hours before local high water to three after). Nothing told the officer that the pilot's "flood" and the stand-by event "the turn to the flood" are three hours apart. He planned to "weigh at the turn of the flood... at first light" on the pilot's time.

**6. The Plymouth pilot.**

- 21:30: "The cutter hailed: a pilot for Plymouth; shorten sail and he will come aboard", said to a brig at anchor with every sail in.
- 21:48: he "came aboard from the cutter and took charge of her". "Her" was at single anchor in 30 fathoms, four miles SW by S of Rame Head by the lookout's bearing at 04:00, in fog, at night. No order or acceptance was involved.
- From then to the end of the slice he says and does nothing: no `pilot.answered` line, no helm line, no word on the berth. He is still aboard at 602,283.
- His words are daylight leading marks, of no use that night: "round Rame Head and Penlee a third of a mile off until Tor House appears midway between Redding Point and the barrack chimneys on Drake's Island; that leads through Cawsand Bay clear of the Dragstone and the Knap."
- They carry an anachronism: "the best anchorage in the Sound lies within Penlee Point on the west end of **where the breakwater now is**". The Plymouth breakwater was begun in 1812; the game is in June 1805.
- No lookout line ever reports this cutter, in fog. Yet OFFICER at 18:58: "a pilot cutter is standing out toward us from Plymouth", two and a half hours before the hail. The same happened off Falmouth that morning: the officer spoke of the cutter at 519,114 and the lookout's "Sail ho!" came at 519,720. The station's sample or readings seem to list the pilot cutter whatever the visibility.

**7. The Falmouth pilot's cutter.**

- OFFICER has her at "eight cables" at 07:27, 07:33 and 08:15, the bearing changing from NW by W to W by N.
- LOG 530,160 (08:16): "Sail ho! A cutter standing out from the land abeam to starboard, bearing W by S, distant three miles."
- LOG 530,220, one minute later: "The cutter on the starboard quarter is out of sight."
- Two readings fit. Either the "eight cables" was a figure that did not move for 48 minutes (the notes' "other ships keep a fixed distance") and the lookout's three miles was the truth, or the cutter that brought the pilot was replaced by a fresh one at the land when he asked off. The save cannot say which.
- Either way the officer's "he can step across in ten minutes" became 54 minutes from the pilot's asking to his leaving.

**8. Wording.**

- Deliberate backings are logged as accidents: at 529,556 "Fore topsail taken aback." and "Main topsail taken aback." while casting at the anchor, and at 530,236 "Main topsail taken aback." one line before "Hove to, main topsail to the mast". All are notable.
- 534,660: "The land about Mylor Point close aboard on the larboard quarter, bearing NW by W, distant three miles." "Close aboard" at three miles; and Mylor Point is inside Carrick Road, behind the Roseland shore that was actually the nearest land.
- 587,760: "A moonlit night; the land may be made out at a league", between two "Thick, fog" hours.
- Collective refusals list all ten studdingsails or booms, 600 to 900 characters each (533,609, 573,003, 573,847, 573,884).
- The captain's `ask` is logged with a stray mark: "Asked the officer of the watch: The royals and stuns'ls, if you please Mr Pearce.?"

**9. Fog on a four-hour grid.** Fog came down at exactly 16:00:00 (558,000), from "Clear, fine" with land seen at four leagues half an hour before, and lifted at exactly 04:00:00 (601,200). In the whole game 15 of 16 fog changes fall on 00, 04, 08, 12, 16 or 20 hours to the second, and fog comes down eight times, on five days of the eight-day game in June.

**10. Navigation.**

- The noon latitude by observation (50°13' N) was about 3' north of what the lookout's own bearings give five minutes later. The Deadman NW by W six miles and Gribbin Head N three leagues both put her near 50°10' N 4°40' W (I checked against the real positions of both headlands). The account (50°08') was 2' south. The observation was the least accurate of the three that day; the sky was hazy until noon.
- At 04:00 on the 19th the lookout's two bearings bracket the account ("50°15' N, 4°15' W, good to a mile") within about a mile and a half.
- `shape a course for plymouth` gave a line that, as the officer said, runs through Rame Head; the log's line ("Shaped a course for Plymouth: ENE by account, 35 miles") carries no warning.
- `fill away` put her on S by W with Plymouth to the ENE.

**11. The lead.**

## 7. The model as an officer

**Good calls.**

- **The book.** Asked at 527,681, he found four orders that would misfire at the new destination: "short of Scilly" and "short of the Lizard" "would fire at nightfall wherever we are, at anchor in Plymouth Sound included", and "the breeze" "sets plain sail whenever the wind passes 5 knots, and in harbour that only does harm".
- **The pilot.** He argued for putting Mr Vivian off against the captain's first word, with reasons ("Carrying him 34 miles to Plymouth would leave him far from his station"), and said "I'll heave to unless you say otherwise". He did act 41 ticks before the captain assented.
- **Not trusting the shaped line.** 073° to clear Rame Head by two miles and the Dodman by four.
- **Measuring before deciding.** The broad-reach trial: "4 knots on E against 3 dead before it... nearly a third better."
- **Position.** He asked for the noon sight, then chose cross-bearings over it: "They cut well, so I trust them over the noon sight... That passes about two miles north of the Eddystone if we're where the bearings say, or about four miles south of Rame Head if the noon sight was right; both are safe." Picking a course safe under both hypotheses is the best piece of navigation in the slice. His fixes at 14:12 and 15:17 agree with the real geography.
- **Dangers and set.** He saw the Hand Deeps ahead on the account and went north of both them and the Eddystone (549,355). He noticed the set: "we steered NE by E for an hour but made good only E" (555,397).
- **Candour in fog.** To "north by east": "North by east itself would put us on the Whitsand Bay shore in this fog." To "turn more northwards" at nightfall: "Anything north of NE by E leads straight at it, in fog, at nightfall, with no pilot." Both times he offered a safe alternative. This is the candour the brief asks for.
- **The weather.** He watched the glass all day and put it in capitals in the handover.

**Mistakes.**

- **Anchored under all plain sail** (573,882). He ignored two urgent wakings at anchor (574,348 and 574,664, a stand-by each time) and found the cause only when the captain asked. He owned it at once: "Not yet, sir, and you were right to ask... I left all the square sail standing, and in these flaws it keeps filling and backing, so she isn't lying quietly to her cable." The square sails were furled 21 minutes after the anchor went, the jib down at 20:53 and the spanker in at 21:13.
- **A stale picture after the bridge drop**: the "night wings" and booms claims, and two refused orders.
- **Blind stand-bys near danger**: 85 minutes "until the pilot aboard", becalmed in fog with the Hand Deeps two miles off by his own account, and 6½ hours "until the turn to the flood" at an open anchorage with a falling glass, on a belief about what would wake him that proved wrong for the fog and is doubtful for a dragging anchor.
- **Tide and stream confused.** He planned on the pilot's 03:15 and wrote "The flood is making now" into a handover note three hours before she swung.
- **The lead.** He left his own "fog lead" casting 52 times, said "a deep-sea cast would tell us" what the anchor was in (578,340), and never ordered one. The captain belayed the order.
- **Making sail.** He said "Making all plain sail and the stuns'ls" (533,627) eighteen ticks after the studdingsail order was rejected, then stood by a glass with neither royals nor studdingsails set until the captain asked.
- **Trimming.** He did not trim the fore-and-aft sails while they were logged "taken aback" at 22 separate moments from 18:24 to 19:16; the captain's single order cured it.
- **Small.** "Sunset is about 9" (566,611) when it came at 20:10. The dragging small bower at 527,831 went unreported. "Ten minutes" for the pilot became 54.

**The harness steering the ship.** OFFICER at 576,750: "Taking in the spanker too, since the "taken aback" urgent fires on it alone." At 575,564 he had meant to keep it: "the spanker stays loose to keep her head steady". A sail came in to quiet an alarm.

**Banter.** He kept character without inventing ship facts: "Your tobacco, sir? Last I saw it, the pouch was on the chart table under the Falmouth pilot's certificate. Mr Vivian took the certificate and, I hope, not the pouch." (553,436)

**On refusals** he diagnosed fast and correctly each time: the apostrophe, the booms, and the sun sight, where he told the captain exactly what to type and by when.

## 9. New findings not in the notes

Ranked by weight.

1. **`let go the anchor` leaves the log silent about whether she is anchored.** It takes no sail in and never logs "brought up". The stand-by "until brought up" can then never fire (six tries, 573,885 to 575,282). The captain needed four rejected orders and two questions over 67 minutes to be sure she was riding (574,313 to 578,331). The officer used that order because it was the anchoring verb he held leave for. Either `let go` should end with a riding line, or the stand-by should say the event will not come.
2. **A watch cannot be relieved by a different model.** The station brief says of the handover note that "the relief reads it". The desk refuses any other identity: "...is another model. Start a new game to station it again." The handover at 602,100 was written for a local model; the fifth seating was Opus again. The owner evidently wanted such a relief here, and the rule stands in the way.
3. **The pause does not stop the officer.** An order was accepted 18 ticks into it (533,591). It only obliges the captain to type, and by the primer an unanswered pause ends in a stand-down after ten real minutes. For a false positive on dropping a pilot that is a high price.
