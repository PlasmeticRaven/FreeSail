# 15. Other sail

Chapter 14 brought the first other vessel into the game, the pilot cutter coming off. This chapter puts a dozen more on the sea and says what the ship knows of any of them, which is what her lookout can see; what the captain may ask and order about a sail in sight; and what a world order is, which is the one kind of order the captain cannot give. The rules are the two that run through the whole of the game. **Everything outward arrives through something the ship models**: a sail reaches her through the lookout and nothing else, and what the lookout cannot make out the captain does not know. **The world keeps the truth and the captain keeps his account**: the world knows where every ship is and what she is about; the captain has a bearing, a distance by estimation and whatever the tops have made out, and the chart draws no more than that.

## What is on the sea

The other sail are hulls from the same ship files as yours (the frigate, the topsail schooner, the cutter, the brig, the lugger and the smack of chapter 2), each with a nation whose colours she wears or does not show, a captain with a goal and a plan. The brig has two descriptions in her one file, since the type served two trades: the brig-sloop of the Navy, sixteen ports a side, and the merchant brig of the trade, deep laden. A goal is one of a few a captain of the period had: trading a route, bound from one place for another, patrolling off a station, running home, carrying the mail, carrying a letter to the ship. The plan is the legs the goal makes, and she sails them at the speed her own file gives for her course in the wind she has, with the tide's stream in her track as it is in yours; a mark to windward she beats for. She is moved once a game minute, at the roll-up's cadence, and no oftener, which is what keeps a dozen of them cheap. Within two miles of you she becomes a nearer thing, with a heading that swings and a speed by the second, and a captain who keeps his course and does no more.

**A ship with a captain of her own** (chapter 17 has the captain). A ship put on the sea with an **intent** beside her goal (`intent: run home to Brest` in the scenario file's entry, or at the end of the world order) carries a rules-based captain who sees from her own masthead, judges by the doctrine of his role and acts: he makes out a stranger and closes her, chases what he can fight and hauls off from what he cannot, heaves to in a gale with sea room and claws off a lee shore, runs for the nearest open road in thick weather, keeps a station, does his business in port and sails again. Her captain's doctrine is by her description: a frigate's is the King's ship's, a brig-sloop's or a Navy cutter's on a station the cruiser's (a small man-of-war, who runs from a frigate and chases a schooner), a letter's the packet's, the rest the merchant's. He sees what his lookout could see and no more: a sail at the horizon his masthead and hers give, within the visibility, her rig at four miles, her colours at two; your ship is a sail to him like any, her colours taken as shown. Nothing of his mind is in your log, only what the lookout sees her do. A ship without an intent sails her plan as before. **Within four miles of you such a ship is awake**: her hull and rig under the same physics as yours, her company at their stations, her captain's orders in her own log through her own channel; beyond six miles she sleeps, her state whole, her plan carrying her and her log kept, which the tops cannot tell apart, since the same brain sails her awake and asleep. Three such ships awake at once is the clock's default allowance (a scenario may set another), a ship in her dealings with a port first, then a hull with a stationed person, then the nearest; a ship that is chasing you or within hail before a nearer one that is not. The far ships do not take the ground: a captain's plan across the land is refused when the scenario loads, and a ship without a captain whose straight leg crosses a cape is said so with the scenario's lines when the game starts.

**The world's business.** A free scenario that says `business: on` (the four merchant ships' free games do; a number, `business: 0.5`, thins it or thickens it) has its region peopled at the start, under the seed, beside the sail it names. **Traders**: a cargo carried from a port of the region where its price is low to one where it is high, by the ports' own price lists as of the day, under her own flag where the port she is bound for takes it and a neutral's (an American, a Dane, a Portuguese) where it does not; some lying in the ports, bound away after their business there, some coming in at the chart's edges for a port, and some loading for a market beyond the region, who sail out past its edge and are gone. **Fishermen**: a smack or a lugger of the port nearest a ground the chart knows (a bank, a bottom of ten to eighty fathoms off the coast), working it for her trip's hours and running home, where she goes in. **Cruisers**: a sloop or a cutter of each nation at war on a station her doctrine names (off the Black Rocks, off Ushant, off the Isle of Bas). Every one has an intent and a captain of her own, and is a sail to your lookout like any other; once an hour another trader may come in at an edge or sail from a port, and a fisherman put out. A ship whose business is done goes in at her port or leaves at the edge. A trader's bargain at her port is struck at the port's prices as yours is. The recorded passages and the trials say nothing and have none, so that they are the ships they name. **The talk on the quay**: where the business is on, the boat you send ashore for the prices brings back, after the list, two or three words of prices high or low at the other ports the market knows, true as of the day and said as rumour ("The talk on the quay, as the boat's crew heard it: brandy dear at Plymouth and wine cheap at Brest. Rumour, no more.").

None of this is yours to read. There is no reading that gives another ship's position, her course by the truth, or her captain's intention. What you have is below.

## The lookout's words for a sail

A sail is seen from the masthead at the distance the horizon gives for the lookout's height of eye and her masthead's, within the weather's visibility and by day (at night a sail is seen only close aboard, as the land is). From a frigate's topmast head that is about twenty-five miles for another frigate's royals, twenty-three for a brig's, twenty-two for a cutter's. The first line is the hail, a bearing first and notable, so a standing order can act on it and a watcher wakes:

```
Sail ho! A sail on the larboard bow, bearing SW by S, distant three leagues.
```

Then, as she nears, the tops make out more of her at the period's distances, each a routine line as it changes: her rig and her course within four miles, her colours or their want within two, what she is within a mile and a half. Every such line gives where she lies from your head, her bearing by compass and the course she stands, so that a line read alone tells you where she is going:

```
The sail on the larboard bow, bearing SW by S, is a brig, standing to the eastward (E by N), under plain sail.
The brig on the larboard bow, bearing SW by S, standing to the eastward (E by N), under plain sail, shows British colours, the red ensign.
The brig on the larboard bow, bearing SSW, is a merchant brig, deep laden, standing to the eastward (E by N), under plain sail; British colours, the red ensign.
```

A ship that shows no colours is said so, and that is all the lookout can say of her nation: "The brig on the larboard bow is a stranger, her colours not made out." A sail that goes over the horizon is lost: "The sail on the larboard quarter is out of sight." The pilot cutter of chapter 14 is one of these vessels and follows the same rules: "a cutter standing out from the land" at four miles, "the Falmouth pilot's cutter" within a mile and a half.

`the strangers` (also `what sail is in sight`) is every sail in sight, nearest first, each by her bearing, her distance by estimation and what has been made out of her so far; `a sail in sight` is the shorter row of chapter 14, in sight or not with the nearest sail's words. The distance by estimation is the lookout's judgement, a sixth out either way as the land's is, drawn once a sighting and held while she makes no way (chapter 12); the chart in the browser draws each sail at that bearing and that distance from the reckoned position, with a bar of doubt along the bearing that grows with the estimate, and the words beside it. Nothing draws where she truly is.

```orders frigate plain-sail
the strangers
what sail is in sight
a sail in sight
```

## The glass on a near ship

Nearer still, the glass shows what she is doing, by tiers of her distance in daylight and clear weather: within two miles what any seaman reads at a look, under way or at anchor, on which tack, hove to, tacking or wearing, running, her sail set or shortening; within eight cables the work on deck and aloft, the hands reefing, making or taking in sail, a boat hoisting out or in, the hands at the nets or the lines, the anchor coming up; within four cables the evolution's own words, as the log would say them of your own ship. A ship awake (within four miles) gives the true evolution; a ship asleep what her plan and her sail give. The visibility bounds each tier, at the twilight the glass reaches a tier less, and by night only her light tells anything, if she shows one: a fishing vessel at her nets hangs a lantern in her rigging, a King's ship cruising shows none. The lookout says the glass unasked when she comes within its tiers and when what it shows of her changes, not oftener than every five minutes (a ship running before it is read on neither tack), and `the glass on the brig` (or `the glass on` with her name or the sail) asks it:

```
The glass on the brig-sloop of war abeam to starboard: hove to on the larboard tack.
The glass aloft makes out the brig abeam to starboard, bearing E: a brig-sloop of war, sixteen ports a side, lying to; a stranger, her colours not made out. The glass shows her hove to on the larboard tack, main topsail aback.
```

The first at a mile, the second at four cables, a brig-sloop lying to east of the frigate with the wind in the west; by night at four cables: "The glass on the sail abeam to starboard: no light shows; nothing of her work is to be made out in the dark." A fisherman near you, crewed, is read the same way: "The glass on the fishing smack on the starboard bow: under way on the starboard tack, with the wind abeam, her boat at the seine.", then "hove to on the starboard tack, her boat at the seine" as she lies by while it fishes (chapter 21).

The tiers' distances are judgement on the period's day glass at sea.

## Making her out, and the chase

`make her out` sends a glass aloft for the nearest sail, or for the one you name by what the tops have called her (`make out the brig`, `make out the stranger`), and answers at once with what the distance allows: the glass reaches half as far again as the eye, so a sail at five miles is a brig to the glass and a sail to the eye, and a sail at nine miles is nothing more to either, hull down. The answer is the same line the lookout would give in time, and the event `a stranger's colours made out` fires for the book when it is her colours the glass makes out.

`give chase` (also `chase the stranger`, `chase the brig`) puts the helm for her bearing, which is Luce's rule for a chaser, to keep the chase on the same compass bearing and so attain her in the shortest time (Luce 1884 p. 553). Given again for the same sail, it reads how her bearing has drawn since the last order and leads the course off the bearing by what brings the drift to nothing, worked from the drift, the lookout's estimate of her distance and your own speed and course, as the master would work it on the slate; the line says how far the bearing drew and how far the course is led, and it never puts her head nearer the wind than she lies close-hauled. It is a helm order and no more: the chase runs on at her plan's speed, you make what sail you please, and the book gives the order again every glass and as the tops make out more of her, which is how the naval cruise's book chases its stranger. Within four cables of a vessel the log says she is within hail, with her course and her colours: the hail is chapter 23's and the guns chapter 22's; the boat sent to her and the prize are not yet in the game.

```orders frigate plain-sail
# rejected: make her out
# rejected: give chase
# rejected: chase the brig
```

The three are refused here because no sail is in sight; at sea with one in sight they are taken. The book's forms for a sail:

```orders frigate plain-sail
standing order "a sail": at a sail sighted then make her out
standing order "her colours": at a stranger's colours made out then give chase
standing order "lost": at a sail lost then shape a course for Falmouth
standing order "within hail": at a sail within hail then heave to
```

## What a world order is, and why you cannot give one

The scenario files put the other sail on the sea, and may change the world as the day goes on: a weather system appended or a waypoint added to one; a ship put on the sea with a goal, or her goal changed; a letter left at a port or sent out by a cutter; a port closed to a nation or a price moved; a person named aboard or ashore; a point of interest put somewhere in the world (`mark: a wreck, her masts showing above the water at 49 58 N 5 16 W`), which the lookout sees as a mark when it comes in sight, its description, bearing and distance in his line. Each is a **world order**, an order to the world and not to the ship, and the scenario gives it at a time (`world_orders:` in `data/scenarios/*.yaml`); and the director (chapter 20) gives them in play. Every world order is journaled at its tick with its source and written in the log at the driver's mark, so that a voyage replays exactly and can be read afterwards for what was arranged; that line is for the record only: the stations' samples and the browser's play log leave it out, so that nobody aboard learns of the cutter before her sail is hailed:

```
World order (the scenario): a cutter sent from Plymouth with a letter from the port admiral.
```

A world order can send the cutter; it cannot put the letter on the cabin table. Everything it causes reaches the ship through what she models: the brig it puts on the sea is a sail the lookout hails, the letter it sends comes aboard by the cutter within hail and is carried aft by the midshipman to wherever the captain is, the price it moves is in the next list the boat brings off, and the person it names ashore is one the boat may fetch. Nothing it does is a line in your log from nowhere. The `ship:` order takes an intent after the goal (`..., bound for the Start, no colours, intent: run home to Brest`), `ship "Palinure": intent <words>` gives a ship on the sea a captain or changes his mind, and two orders are the director's seat hook (`seat: <model> as captain of <ship> for <n> samples`, `unseat: <ship>`), refused without a consent record, the owner's grant and budget.

The captain cannot give one. The grammar knows the channel's words and refuses them at the prompt in so many words, which is the game's promise that the captain plays the ship and not the world. **The owner can**: typed with the prefix `world:` at the console's prompt or in the browser's order box (`world: ship "Palinure": intent run home to Brest`), the order is the owner's to the world, journaled with its source, (`docs/agents/Harness.md`, section 17); the order itself is not shown in the play log, only its effects as the ship meets them.

```orders frigate plain-sail
# rejected: ship: a brig "Harpy" of France at the Iroise, running home to Brest
# rejected: weather: low "the low" radius 450 km at 1805-06-13T04:00 -300 500 990
# rejected: port brest: closed to the United States
# rejected: message: at Brest from the Prefect maritime: "The captain is begged to dine."
# rejected: person: agent "Mr Fox" ashore at Brest
# rejected: mark: a cask adrift at 49 58 N 5 16 W
# rejected: world order: a gale tonight
```

## The two passages

Two passages ship with the game: **the merchant passage** (`data/scenarios/merchant-passage.yaml`), the schooner from Falmouth for Brest with forty tons of tin bought at the quay, out on the ebb, a sail sighted off the Lizard and not made out, the Iroise in her chart's words at the deep-sea lead's cast, the Goulet refused on the ebb on the pilot's word and taken on the flood, Brest entered by the pilot and the tin sold; and **the naval cruise** (`naval-cruise.yaml`), the frigate from Plymouth with her chronometer and the yard's provisions to the station off Ushant, kept two days under her book, a stranger sighted and chased, a message by the cutter. Each has its own book beside it (`.orders`), which is what the scenario loads; the starter book of chapter 11 is a choice, loaded at any time with `read the standing orders from data/standing_orders/starter.orders`, and neither passage needs it.

## Where it comes from

The other captains are chapter 17's rules-based captain on the far body (the doctrines in `data/captains/`). The look-out is Falconer 1780, LOOK-OUT ("a watchful attention to some important object ... land, rocks, enemies"), and the colours his COLOURS ("the flags or banners which distinguish the ships of different nations"); the chase is Luce 1866 ch. XXXIII, 'Chasing' ("the officers in charge of a vessel, either chasing or chased, should constantly take the bearings, and angle subtended by the masts of the other"), and Luce 1884 p. 553 ("by constantly keeping the chase on the same compass bearing, the chaser will attain the chase in the shortest time possible"); the horizon is 2.08 times the root of the heights in metres (Bowditch's 1.17 times the root in feet) with each ship's masthead from her file; the distances at which a rig, the colours and a ship herself are made out are judgement, as are the pace of a boat under oars and the level of detail's two miles. The polar a far-detail ship sails by is her file's own, the sails model of this game balanced against her hull's resistance at each point of sailing, within half a knot of what she makes when sailed.
