# 26. The boat, and going aboard another ship

Chapter 14 sent the boat ashore on her errands and chapter 15 put the other sail on the sea. This chapter takes you down into the boat yourself, and from her, or by her, aboard another ship; it says what the readings are while you are there, how you come home, and what the ship does meanwhile. Everything in it is an order or a reading of the game's own; where the period's practice is followed, the book that gives it is named.

## The boat as a vessel

A ship's boat with a file of her own (`the boats` lists each boat she carries with her length, oars and crew; those with a file are the frigate's launch and first cutter, the brig's launch, cutter and jolly boat, the merchant brig's jolly boat, the cutter's boat and the lugger's boat) can be manned and commanded as a vessel of the sea. The rest keep to their errands by the script of chapter 14 and cannot be taken: the refusal says so.

```
heave to
go to the launch
```

`go to the boat` (or `go to the launch`, `go to the cutter`, by her name) hoists her out and mans her, her coxswain at the yoke lines and one man to an oar, and puts you in her, lying off the lee quarter. **She is hoisted out hove to or at anchor**: with two knots of way on the ship the order is refused ("heave to or come to an anchor before the boat is hoisted out"), which is the practice of every book (Luce 1866, 'Boats'). From then the prompt is hers and the readings are hers: `the speed` is her way, `the wind` the wind at her gunwale, `in sight` what a man standing in a boat sees (his eye a little under two metres over the water, so that a ship's hull goes under his horizon at five miles), and `where am I` says so:

```
Where am i: in the Amazon's launch, 10 oars out, giving way together, the men 0.2 hours at the oars.
```

**Her orders are a boat's.** `give way` (or `give way together`) is the full stroke: a launch of ten oars makes three knots and a half fresh, a four-oared boat about the same for her size. `easy all` is a light stroke, half the pull. `lie on your oars` holds her with the oars out and no stroke; `boat your oars` lays them in; `out oars` ships them again. `pull for the quay`, `pull for the ship`, `pull for the Medusa` (a sail in sight by her name), `pull for NE` (a point of the compass) or `pull for 50 08 N 5 02 W` gives the coxswain his mark, and he steers for it as it bears while the men give way. **The men tire by the hour**: the stroke they pull at the end of the first hour is four fifths of the fresh one, at the end of four hours half, and they recover slowly lying on their oars; `where am I` tells the hours. A boat under oars is a vessel like any other to the physics: her way comes off her when the oars are boated, she carries her way into a turn, and the tide sets her as it sets the ship.

**Her sail.** Each of these boats carries one mast, laid along the thwarts, and a standing lug. `step the mast` gets it up ("the mast stepped and the lug bent to its yard"); `set the lug` hoists the sail, which is the sail orders' own; `take in the lug` lowers it; `unstep the mast` lays the mast down again. The oars cannot be pulled with the lug drawing, nor the lug set with the mast down, and the refusals say which order comes first, as the practice was. Under her lug the boat sails as any lugger does, close-hauled or running, and her coxswain tends the sheet to the wind as a boat's sheet is tended, held in hand and never belayed, for a boat's lug set flat with the wind abaft the beam lies aback and will not steer. `pull for ...` under the lug gives the coxswain the mark to steer for and leaves the oars in.

```
step the mast
set the lug
pull for the ship
```

**Coming alongside.** `come alongside` (or `the boat alongside`, or `back to the ship`) makes for the ship under whatever she is under, and within a boat's length she is hoisted in: her people come aboard, you are at your own station again with the ship's readings, and the lines of her log are kept in the ship's log under her name and the hour, so that what the boat saw is in the ship's record. The ship's `the boat` reading says `the launch at the booms` once more. `send the boat ashore` and the boat's other errands run as before, with nobody in her and no change to their times.

**The boat lost.** A sea above her weather swamps a boat that is out in it (a short or heavy sea for a boat under twenty feet, a heavy sea for a launch), and her people with her; from the ship, `cut the boat adrift` loses her by order, refused while there are people in her. A boat lost is sent on no errand and manned by nobody, the reading says so (`the boat lost: swamped on the 12th; a new one is the yard's`), until `demand a boat of the yard` at a port with a yard puts a new one at the booms in two days for twenty-five pounds.

## The tow

Becalmed, or in an air too light to steer by, `tow her with the boat` hoists the boat out and manned for the tow, the hawser's bight to her stern-sheets and the boat a line's length ahead pulling with every oar (Luce 1866, 'Boats', 'Towing': "the heaviest boats should always be nearest the tow"). `tow her with the boat to the NE`, or a bearing in degrees, says the direction; ahead when none is said. **The ship's head comes round first**, in a few minutes, the helmsman following the boat, and then she gathers way against her resistance: about a knot for a cutter or a lugger, and for a frigate under her launch alone a little under a knot, in each case falling as the men tire by the hour, which the log says once an hour ("the boat's people are tiring; her way is falling"). `the boat alongside` ends it: the line cast off, the boat hoisted in, the ship's way left to die. The tow is refused in a sea the boat cannot work in, at anchor ("weigh first") and aground, and it ends of itself if the ship comes to an anchor or takes the ground. The kedge (chapter 13) is laid out by the boat as before, and she is said away with it until she is hoisted in.

## Aboard another ship

A person of the ship goes aboard another in the boat: `send the boat to the Medusa with Mr Veale` carries him to a ship lying within a boat's pull (a mile), and `send the boat to the Medusa for Mr Veale` fetches him home (chapter 14 has the forms). There he is **a visitor**: he keeps his own station's name and voice, with a passenger's reach aboard her (her readings, the conversation where he stands, his journal, `where am I`, which answers "aboard the Medusa, on the quarterdeck, a visitor, of the Amazon"), gives none of her deck's orders and none of his own ship's from there, and has his own station whole again when he comes home. The boarders' officer who leads a party onto a deck won (chapter 25) stands on that deck the same way, and a captive put aboard a captor is stood down with the reason.

**The player on another ship.** The game may be started with you aboard another ship (`--seat captain --ship Medusa` at the console or the browser): your own ship is left as she lies, her watch kept and her sails as they were, under her rules captain if the scenario gives her an intent, with no captain's hand on her otherwise, and the log says so; the prompt is the other ship's, her rules captain standing aside at your hand and taking her back at `captain: carry on`; `follow "the ship"` brings the readings and the prompt home. The reading `the followed ship` says which ship the readings are, and `the ship` names her. A world with no ship of the player's at all (the scenario's `ship: none`, or `director: observe`) runs its business under its own captains with the readings of the sea and the sky and nothing of a ship's; `follow "<name>"` takes the readings to any ship on the sea, `follow nobody` gives the view back, and `look at <position>` moves it.

## What the director does with the boat

The director's forms (chapter 20) reach her: `person: "Mr Fox" goes aboard the Medusa by the boat` puts a person of any ship aboard another by his ship's boat, where the two lie within a boat's pull; `follow "the Medusa"` moves the drivers to her; `aboard: the boat stove`, `aboard: the boat swamped` (each doubted on a smooth sea) and `aboard: the boat gone adrift` lose the boat with whoever is in her; and the truth shows a manned boat as a vessel of the sea with her ship named, who is in her, whether she is under oars or her lug, and whom she tows. A station standing by may wake at `the boat manned`, `the boat lost`, `the tow begun`, `the tow ended` and `aboard another ship`.
