# 13. The tide and the anchor

Chapter 10 gave the master his reckoning and chapter 12 his longitude; both left the sea standing still. This chapter puts the tide under the ship and the stream in her track, says what a captain of 1805 knew of either and how he reckoned it, and then brings her to an anchor and gets her under way again, in Luce's words of command. The rule throughout is decision 29's: **the world keeps the truth and the captain keeps his account.** The world's tide rises and falls at the ship every minute, by the harmonic constants of eleven tide gauges; the captain's tide is the hour of high water he works from his epitome's establishment and the age of the moon, which is all the period had. No reading gives him the world's; his own is a reading, in his own words.

## The world's tide

Wherever the world has a chart (a scenario with a `region`), it has a tide. The height above the chart's datum, which is low water of spring tides, is under the lead's cast and over every rock that covers; the stream runs through the ship's track as the water's own velocity, so that she makes her way through the water as she always did and goes over the ground by her way and the stream's together. The log-line, which measures through the water, never sees it; the reckoning run from the log is wrong by it, which is what the period's reckonings were wrong by, and the lead and the land put right.

Three things of the world's tide a captain can see for himself, and the game gives him:

- **The height, in the lead.** A cast in the Roads at high water of springs reads five metres more than the chart's datum; at low water the chart's own figure. The master, laying his cast on the chart, takes off his own allowance for it (below), and looks within his doubt for the depth his corrected cast says, not the world's.
- **The rocks that cover.** A rock the pilot says "shows at half-tide" is in sight at low water and gone at high; the lookout does not hail what the water hides, and the grounding reads the tide over it. The Manacles keep "one head always above water" and are seen at any tide; the Rose, "thirteen feet on them at low water", is never seen and takes a frigate's keel at the bottom of a spring tide and not at the top.
- **The set.** A ship hove to off the Lizard for six hours goes east or west by the stream's miles, which the log never shows; `state` shows her over the ground, and the account shows where the master thinks she is.

The stream runs by areas the pilots describe: in Carrick Road along the channel at a knot and a half at springs, off the Lizard east from three hours before local high water to three after at two knots at springs and one at neaps, west after, with a slack at the turn; through the Fromveur off Ushant at seven knots. The turn of the tide is an event the ship's log says when she is at anchor (`the turn of the tide`), since she swings to it; under way it is in her track alone.

## The captain's tide

The captain has an epitome (Norie's in a ship of war, Moore's in a merchantman; the scenario may say `epitome: moore`) with a table of the establishments of the ports: the hour of high water on the day of full and change of the moon, Falmouth 5h 15m, Plymouth 5h 33m, Brest 3h 48m, St Malo 6h 00m. From it and the moon's age he works today's high water by Moore's rule: the age times forty-eight minutes is the moon's southing past noon, and the establishment added to it is the hour of high water; the other tide is twelve hours and twenty-five minutes before or after. The age he takes from the almanac to the quarter of a day. The reading is his, in his words:

```
High water at Falmouth about a quarter to three in the middle watch and a quarter past
three in the afternoon by the epitome (Falmouth 5h 15m at full and change, the moon
thirteen days old; the rise 15 feet at springs)
```

`the tide by the almanac` (also `the tide`, `high water by the almanac`) gives it for the nearest place in his table to the account, or for a port by name. Where his table has no such place it says so and gives the nearest it has. The rule's errors are the period's: on the day of full moon his high water is within an hour of the world's, and at the quarters up to an hour out, which is why the Admiralty wanted tide tables and why Whewell was at work on them.

His allowance for the tide under the lead is worked the same way, by his own book and never the sea's: the rise at springs his table gives for the nearest place to his account, two-thirds of it at the quarters, and the height now above low water at springs (his chart's datum) by the hours from his high water, which is the rule of twelfths worked exactly. Every place of both tables has its rise: where the period's own table gives none (Moore's gives none at all), the table carries the round figure his sailing directions would give, marked judgement in the data. It is a rule of thumb against the world's harmonic tide and it is wrong by a foot or two, and by a fathom when his hour is an hour out near half tide; that is the master's error, and the lead's cast carries it onto the chart. When what he takes off is a fathom or more, the cast's line says it: "By the deep eleven; loose sand. Two fathoms of tide allowed by the epitome: nine fathoms on the chart. The account kept." Where the cast so reduced answers nothing within his doubt, he keeps his account and says the cast does not agree ([chapter 10](10-the-reckoning.md), the lead).

The fall he reckons to low water from where she lies, which he gives the captain letting go an anchor (below), is the same tide counted from the day's own low water and not from the springs': at the neaps it is the less.

## The tide in the reckoning

The master works the tide into his reckoning himself, as one more course of the traverse. He has three things to work it from, and all three are books: the hour of high water at the nearest place in his epitome, by the rule above; the moon's age from his almanac, which tells him springs from neaps; and what his sailing directions say of the water his *account* puts her in. The directions state a stream as the period's books do: the point the flood sets toward, the rate at springs to the half knot and at neaps half of that, and the hour of the tide at which it runs strongest. Off the Lizard: the flood sets east, two knots and a half at springs, strongest at high water. In the Iroise: north-east by north, a knot and a half, strongest two hours and a half before high water. In the Goulet: east by north, four knots and a half. Beyond the waters his directions cover he allows nothing.

From these he reckons the stream by the quarter hour and carries its sum into the account with the run, under way, hove to and becalmed alike. He never sees the world's tide: his high water may be an hour from the sea's, his rate is a book's round figure, and where his account has put her in the wrong water he allows the wrong stream. His tide is nearest the truth where the books are good and the water is of one piece, as in the Iroise; in the open Channel, where Moore's rule can be an hour and a half out and the books say only "north-east", it is a rough allowance and no more. The difference is in his doubt (chapter 10), and it is still the play.

The log says when his tide turns and when, by his account, she passes into other waters; the line is routine, and the turn is an event for the book (`at the turn of the tide by the reckoning`), which is the moment to shape a course again:

```
  Forenoon watch (11:14)  By the master's tide the flood makes: allowing a knot and a half to the NE.
  First dog watch (16:19)  By the master's reckoning she is in the waters off the Lizard: allowing the flood, two knots and a half to the E.
```

The reading `the reckoning` says what is allowed and by whom: "49° 50' N, 4° 59' W by account; the tide allowed: the ebb, three quarters of a knot to the SW, by the directions for the open Channel and high water at the Lizard by the epitome". At anchor it reads "none while she rides at anchor", and beyond his directions "none, in open water".

The captain may put his own set in its place. `allow two knots of set to the west` replaces the master's tide, in the traverse and in every course shaped, from that moment until the captain hands it back with `allow the tide by the book`; `allow no set` tells the master to allow nothing. A set the captain gave on the flood is still allowed on the ebb: nothing turns it but his own order.

```orders frigate
allow two knots of set to the west
allow the tide by the book
```

```
Allowing two knots of set to the west in the reckoning, by the captain's order, in place of the master's own tide.
The tide in the reckoning handed back to the master, by the book: the ebb, three quarters of a knot to the SW, by the directions for the open Channel and high water at the Lizard by the epitome.
```

When she takes the ground the master gives his word by the same tide: "She took the ground on the ebb: she will not float before the flood, and the master makes it half past five in the evening by the epitome." He may be an hour out.

## Taking the ground

The grounding check of chapter 12 now reads the tide: the chart's depth at her bow, her stern and her middle, with the tide's height, against her draught and her heel; and the charted dangers by name, a rock or a ledge with its head above or below the datum as the pilots give it, within its extent of her. When the keel touches:

- **She stops.** Her way through the water is checked at once and the ground holds her; the stream runs past her, which the log-line shows and the reckoning runs no distance for.
- **The masts feel it.** At six knots the blow is one strain of the rating on the topmasts and above, half of it on the lower masts; above that they carry away, from the top down, as any spar does under the strain model. At four knots or less nothing is felt aloft.
- **She makes water.** On rock at four knots the carpenter reports her stove and the well rises a couple of feet an hour; on sand or mud a few inches. The log says the well's rise by the foot, and past two metres in the well she is waterlogged and nothing more is modelled (the pumps are a later milestone's).
- **She floats off** when the tide gives her a foot under her keel: "She is off, and afloat again on the flood." On a falling tide she waits for the next.

The log's line is the one you would expect: "She has taken the ground forward, on good holding ground: two fathoms and a half of water by the chart, and she draws 15 feet; she struck at five knots and a half, heeling 1 degree, the tide falling." The event `aground` (or `the ground taken`) and `afloat` serve the book: `at aground then furl all sail` is a sensible standing order, and `when the anchor is dragging then veer cable` another.

## The ground tackle

Every ship carries anchors by her size, from her file, which the generator writes by Luce's rule of five hundredweight of bower to the hundred tons and Steel's tables for the cables: the frigate two bowers of forty-seven hundredweight, a sheet anchor and a stream anchor, and a kedge, with nineteen-inch cables of a hundred and twenty fathoms (two bent to the best bower); the schooner two bowers of eleven hundredweight and a kedge; the cutter and the brig between. `the ground tackle` says what she has and where each anchor is:

```orders frigate
the ground tackle
```

```
The best bower, 47 cwt, 240 fathoms of 19-inch cable: at the bows. The small bower, 47
cwt, 120 fathoms of 19-inch cable: at the bows. The sheet anchor, 47 cwt, 120 fathoms of
19-inch cable: at the bows. The stream anchor, 12 cwt, 120 fathoms of 12.5-inch cable: at
the bows. The kedge, 6 cwt, 120 fathoms of 9.5-inch cable: at the bows.
```

The anchors are named as the forecastle names them: `the best bower` (the starboard one), `the small bower`, `the sheet anchor`, `the stream anchor`, `the kedge`, and `the second anchor` for whichever bower is not yet down. `the anchor` alone is the one she rides by, or the best bower.

## Coming to an anchor

One order does the whole of Luce's evolution (ch. XXXIV, 'Having a leading wind, to run in and anchor'): all hands are called, the studding sails, royals, topgallants and courses come in, the helm goes down for the stronger of the wind and the tide (she is brought head to a stream over a knot and a half, else head to wind), the topsails are clewed up and the jib hauled down as she comes to, and when her way over the ground is gone, "Stand clear of the cable! Stream the buoy! Let go the best bower!" The cable is veered roundly to five times the depth the lead calls, as far as the cable bent allows; when it comes taut and her way is stopped she is brought up, the spanker is brailed up, and all hands furl sail and square the yards.

```orders frigate plain-sail
come to an anchor
come to an anchor with the small bower
come to an anchor in twelve fathoms
```

The last is the depth to let go in: she stands on, sail shortened, until the lead calls it ("The lead calls twelve fathoms."), and gives the order up in words if it has not in an hour. The scope is its own, and may be said in the same order. The log says each step, and at the end:

```
Brought up by the best bower in 14 fathoms, sixty-nine fathoms of cable; Riding by the
best bower to the flood, wind and tide together.
```

`let go the anchor` lets it go where she is, whatever her way. It is what you do when she is driving onto a lee shore and there is no time for the evolution; the anchor may bring her up and may not. **It says its scope as the order is given, and takes one.** Left to itself it veers five times the depth, as far as the cable bent allows, and its first line says the figure, so that a hundred and thirty fathoms running out in twenty-six is no surprise:

```
  Forenoon watch (10:01)  Order: let go the best bower.
  Forenoon watch (10:01)  Stand clear of the cable; stream the buoy! Let go the best bower in 17 fathoms; veering to eighty-four fathoms, five times the depth.
```

`let go the best bower and veer to sixty fathoms` (or `with sixty fathoms`; `in sixty fathoms` after `let go` is read the same way, as it always was) veers to that and no further, and `come to an anchor and veer to sixty fathoms` likewise:

```
  Forenoon watch (10:01)  Stand clear of the cable; stream the buoy! Let go the best bower in 17 fathoms; veering to sixty fathoms and no further.
* Forenoon watch (10:02)  The best bower let go in 17 fathoms and a half; sixty fathoms of cable veered.
* Forenoon watch (10:04)  Brought up by the best bower in 17 fathoms and a half, sixty fathoms of cable; Riding by the best bower, head to wind.
```

"Brought up" is said for an anchor let go by itself as it is for the whole evolution, when the cable has come taut and her way is gone; it is the event `brought up` for a standing order or a stand-by.

**Three warnings come as the anchor goes**, each a notable line of its own and none of them a refusal. *Her swinging room:* at anchor she swings about it with the wind and the tide, by the cable along the ground and her own length, and when that circle comes within a cable of the nearest land as the lookout judges it, "With eighty-four fathoms out she will swing within a cable of the land to the eastward." (or "onto the land"). A shorter scope, or another berth, is the answer. *Her cable:* when the depth wants more than the cable bent to that anchor, "The small bower has a hundred and twenty fathoms of cable bent to it, and five times the depth wants a hundred and fifty-four fathoms: she will have all of it and no more." *The water at low water:* "By the master's tide there will be two fathoms here at low water, and she draws 15 feet.", when the depth the lead finds, less the fall the master reckons to low water by his own tide, is less than her draught.

```orders frigate
let go the anchor
let go the best bower in twenty fathoms
let go the best bower and veer to sixty fathoms
let go the best bower with sixty fathoms
come to an anchor in twelve fathoms and veer to fifty fathoms
let go the second anchor
veer cable
veer twenty fathoms
veer to ninety fathoms
# rejected: veer to the moon
```

**An anchor's name is honoured.** `veer`, `heave in`, `heave short` and `weigh` work the anchor you name, and the anchor she rides by when you name none; with two anchors down, `veer the small bower to ninety fathoms` veers the small bower's cable and leaves the best bower's alone. "To" is to a scope, wherever the anchor's name stands in the order, and a bare number is so much more: `veer to eighty fathoms`, `veer the best bower to eighty fathoms` and `veer to eighty fathoms on the best bower` all leave eighty out; `veer twenty fathoms` and `veer the small bower twenty fathoms` veer twenty more. A "to" that is less than what is out is refused, with the order that does it: "the best bower has eighty-two fathoms out already; to have less out, heave in to 60 fathoms". An anchor that is not down is refused by name, with the one she rides by: "the sheet anchor is at the bows, not down; she rides by the best bower". Never another anchor in silence.

**`heave in`** is the capstan's order for less cable: `heave in to seventy fathoms` to a scope, `heave in ten fathoms` so much, either with an anchor's name. It stops short of the depth: to heave the anchor off the ground say `weigh`, and for a short stay `heave short`, which takes no number (a number after it is answered with `heave in to`). Moored, heaving in on one cable veers the other as she comes over, and the line says so.

```orders frigate
let go the best bower and veer to eighty fathoms
let go the second anchor
veer the small bower to ninety fathoms
veer to ninety fathoms on the best bower
veer the small bower twenty fathoms
heave in to seventy fathoms
heave in ten fathoms
heave in the small bower to forty fathoms
heave short the small bower
# rejected: heave short twenty fathoms
```

## Riding

At single anchor she lies to the stream when it runs, and to the wind when it is the stronger, and between them when they are across each other: "riding to the flood, the wind across the tide" (Lever's figures of the leeward tide and the windward tide). At the turn of the tide the cable slackens and she swings, which the log says. The physics of it is the cable's pull at the hawse against the anchor's holding, which is some four times its weight in good ground, less in soft or rocky ground, and less as the cable's angle at the anchor rises on a short scope: at five times the depth it holds what the ground allows; at a cable and a half the depth it holds a quarter of that. When the pull exceeds the holding the anchor comes home along the bottom, and after a minute of it the log says so, urgently and once, with those of Luce's three answers that are left to take (not "veer more cable" when the whole of the cable is out, nor an anchor that is down already):

```
! The best bower is dragging: veer more cable; let go the small bower, or back her with the
  stream.
```

While it goes on there is no second urgent line. A notable one says how far she has come, a quarter of an hour apart at the most and only while she is still moving: "The best bower still coming home: a cable since it began." When the anchor has not moved for five minutes it "holds again, having come home a cable and a half", routinely; and the next drag after that is a new one, with its own urgent line. An anchor whose cable is slack is not said to drag.

**The ground decides how much she holds.** The anchor's depth and bottom are read where it lies, from the chart's note: good ground and sand and clay hold as the rule says, mud eight tenths of it, shells and weed six, stones four, rock three. A note of two grounds holds as the mean of them: "rock and mud" in the Goulet a little over half, "sand and rock" off Roscoff two thirds. Every port's road and anchorage has its note, Brest road among them (mud).

`veer cable` gives her more scope; `let go the second anchor` is the other bower; `back the anchor` shackles the stream anchor on the riding cable so that the two hold together. The cable itself is judged as every line is (spec §7.5): above its rating it wears, and the log says "bar-taut and surging on the bitts"; above half its breaking strain it may part, and "the anchor is lost, and she is adrift" is an urgent line.

Two readings tell you how she rides. `the anchor` says down, riding to which tide by which anchor with how much cable out, or aweigh, catted, at the bows, lost, or aground and where. `the cable` says the scope, bar-taut or slack, and the strain against its rating, which is the number the dialect compares (`when the cable exceeds the rating then veer cable`). Both stand in `state` while she is at anchor.

```orders frigate
back the anchor
# rejected: heave short the kedge
```

## Weighing

`heave short` rigs the capstan and heaves the cable in to a short stay, a cable and a half the depth, where she is ready to go the moment the anchor is broken out. `weigh` (also `weigh anchor`, `up anchor`, `get under way`) heaves short if she is not, heaves the cable up and down, breaks the anchor out, heaves it up to the bows, cats and fishes it, and reports her under way; the time is by the scope, at the capstan's rate of a fathom in ten seconds with all hands at the bars. `cat and fish the anchor` secures an anchor left aweigh.

With two anchors down, `weigh the small bower` weighs that one and leaves her riding by the other: the other's cable is veered as she comes over the anchor she is heaving up, and the last line says how she is left, "The small bower catted and fished; she rides by the best bower, sixty fathoms out." `weigh` alone weighs the anchor she rides by. If the cable that is to hold her meanwhile is too short to let her come over the anchor, the order is refused in words that say what would do it: "the best bower cannot be hove up while the small bower holds her: to come over it she must have a hundred and ten fathoms of the small bower's cable, which is a hundred fathoms in all. Weigh the small bower first, or unmoor; she rides by the best bower".

```orders frigate
heave short
weigh
cat and fish the anchor
weigh the small bower
# rejected: weigh twenty fathoms
```

**At anchor she is still a ship.** Sail may be furled, handed or loosed to dry, the yards squared or braced, the topgallant masts and yards sent down or swayed up, a line rove: `furl all sail`, `square the yards`, `brace the yards square`, `loose sails to dry` are taken at anchor and aground as at sea. What waits till she weighs is the helm and the manoeuvres: `steer`, `keep her full`, the conning words, `trim sails`, `tack`, `wear`, `heave to`, `fill away` and their like are answered "She is at anchor; 'steer' must wait till she weighs." (or, aground, "till she floats").

Sail is yours to make: the anchor's evolutions set none, and a ship weighing in a tideway with no sail on her is set by the stream like any other floating thing, as the gate's frigate was found to be the first time the lead tried it. The order of the thing in Luce is sail first, then the anchor: topsails sheeted home and hoisted, then "Heave round!", and the jib run up as the anchor breaks out, so that she casts the way the master wants.

A dragging anchor is an urgent line of the log, once for each dragging: it wakes a station that is standing by, and the clock eases for it. The lines that follow while it goes on are notable, and "holds again" is routine.

The events for the book: `the anchor let go`, `brought up`, `the anchor aweigh`, `the anchor weighed` (or `under way`), `the anchor dragging`, `the cable parted`, `aground` (or `the ground taken`), `afloat`, `the turn of the tide` (and, by which way she swung, `the turn to the flood`, `the turn to the ebb`).
