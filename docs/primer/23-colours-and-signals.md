# 23. Colours, the private signal, a hail, a signal made and read

The voice between ships, in the player's words. Before a gun is fired a captain of 1805 knew whom he had to deal with by three means and no more: the colours she showed, the private signal of the month, and the hail within the trumpet's reach; and he worked his consorts and his convoy by the signal book. The game has each as a thing of the ship's state, seen by the lookout at the range the thing carries, and nothing is refused for being unwise: a shot under false colours is a pirate's act, and the log says so.

The colours, the signals, the private signal, the lights and a hail are **the deck's orders**, as the sails and the helm are (chapter 14): given from the quarterdeck, and refused below and ashore in the same words (*You are in the cabin; go on deck to give an order of the deck.*). Their readings (`the colours`, `the signals`, `the private signal`) are answered wherever you are, with where they are to be had. A visitor aboard another ship may hail and signal from her deck. Passing the word from the cabin for the officer of the watch to make a signal is a later step of the game's.

## The colours

Your colours are a state the other ships' lookouts read: shown (your own ensign), none, false (another nation's, the ruse the law allowed until the first shot), struck, or the ensign union down in distress. The lookout of every ship, far or near, reads the nation *shown*, never the true one, so a French privateer under British colours is "British colours, the red ensign" to the King's captain who makes her out at two miles (three with the glass aloft), and the truth is the director's alone.

```orders cutter
hoist the colours
hoist French colours
hoist false colours
haul down the colours
hoist the ensign union down
the colours
```

```
False colours hoisted: French colours, the tricolour. The ruse is the law's until the first shot; a gun fired under them makes her a pirate to every nation.
Her true colours hoisted: British colours, the red ensign, the false ones down.
The colours: British colours, the red ensign.
```

`hoist false colours` with no nation named shows the principal enemy's. `haul down the colours` shows none; under fire it is the strike by the custom of the sea, and the log says so. `strike the colours` is chapter 22's and never refused. When another sail shifts hers the lookout says it: "The brig hoists her colours: French colours, the tricolour"; "The cutter hauls down her colours." A rules captain shows his true colours before his first shot; a privateer's doctrine closes her stranger under the enemy's colours and shows her own at four cables. If you fire under false or no colours the first shot is told once ("Fired into the brig under false colours: a pirate's act by the law of nations; every ship that saw it and every port that hears of it will hold us so"), the ports' stance turns against you, and every captain whose people had you in sight holds you a pirate from then on, whatever colours you hoist after.

The browser's picture of her flies what her colours say: her ensign at the peak of her aftermost gaff (at an ensign staff over the taffrail where she has no gaff), standing out to leeward of the wind she feels; the ensign of the nation she pretends under false colours; the ensign turned over in distress; nothing with her colours struck or none shown. Hold the pointer over it and it reads as the lookout reads it: "British colours, the red ensign, at the peak". A nation's other flags (the pendant, the jack, a commodore's broad pendant) are drawn at their places as the colours' book gives them.

## The flags she wears

Beyond her colours a ship wears what her service and her captain's place give her, as her nation's colours have it: a King's ship her pendant at the main, the long streamer that marks a man-of-war to every eye that can make out her colours; a commodore his broad pendant in its place; an admiral his flag at the main, the fore or the mizen by his rank; a merchantman her ensign alone, and a privateer hers with her own jack at anchor. At anchor the ensign flies from the staff over the taffrail and the jack from the jackstaff at the bowsprit's end; beating to quarters doubles the ensigns, one at the peak and another at the main topgallant masthead, and sends the jack to the fore, so that no shot can bring her colours down by accident. The lookout reads them at the colours' range, with the colours: "The ship abeam to larboard, bearing SW by W, is a frigate, standing to the north-eastward (NE), under plain sail; British colours, the red ensign; she wears a pendant at the main: a man-of-war." A commodore's ship is read "she wears a broad pendant at the main: a commodore"; a merchantman's ensign alone adds nothing to her colours.

```orders cutter
hoist the broad pendant
hoist a second ensign at the main
hoist the jack
the flags
haul down the jack
strike the broad pendant
lower the second ensign
# rejected: haul down the jack
```

```
A commodore's broad pendant hoisted at the main.
The red ensign hoisted at the main topgallant masthead, the ensigns doubled.
The union jack hoisted at the fore.
The flags: British colours, the red ensign at the peak; a commodore's broad pendant at the main truck; British colours, the red ensign at the main topgallant masthead; the union jack at the fore truck.
The union jack hauled down.
A commodore's broad pendant hauled down.
The second ensign, the red ensign, hauled down from the main; her colours at the peak.
```

`the flags` says what flies where: the ensign, the pendant, the jack, the broad pendant and a second ensign, each at its place, and then the signal flying if a hoist is up (`the colours` reads the ensign alone, as before). The flags you order stay up with the colours and come down with them, or one at a time by name: `haul down the jack`, `strike the broad pendant`, `lower the second ensign`; one not flying is refused in words. They are the deck's orders, as the colours are. Every nation's flags are drawn from her own colours (the British ensigns of the three squadrons with the Union of 1801 in the canton, the pendant and the jack; the French tricolour, blue at the hoist; the Spanish red and yellow of 1785, the red a quarter of the depth above and below; Portugal's white with the arms), and a nation with no colours of her own drawn wears a plain ensign of the colour her flag is named by, and a plain pendant in a ship of war.

## Dressing ship

On a day of rejoicing a ship in harbour dresses: her flags and pendants hung in the rigging, her colours flying as they do. `dress ship` does it in the fashion of the day: about 1805, as Röding's dictionary of 1794 gives it, "hung with flags and pendants made fast to the yards, stays, shrouds, backstays and other rigging", and not yet the line of signal flags from masthead to masthead, which the books give only from the 1880s. A King's ship hangs her ensigns and her jack at the lower yardarms, her book's signal flags at the topsail yardarms, along her stays and in her shrouds, and her pendants at the topgallant yardarms, one of each, so that a ship of few flags leaves some places bare; a merchantman has her colours to hang, and little else. Which flag goes where no book of the period says: a junior officer drew up the scheme for the captain, as the game does for you. She stays dressed until `undress ship`, until sunset, or until her colours come down, and the log says so. The lookout says it of another ship: "she is dressed in her flags".

```orders cutter
dress ship
the flags
undress ship
# rejected: undress ship
```

```
Dress ship: the hands aloft with the flags, and the ship dressed with her flags and pendants in the yards, the stays and the shrouds.
Undress ship: the flags hauled down from the yards and the stays, and stowed.
```

A ship dresses by day with her own colours flying: `dress ship` is refused by night and with her colours down, struck or turned over. It is the deck's order, as the colours are. The browser's picture hangs the flags at their places.

## The private signal

The recognition signal: a small table per nation per month, issued under seal to the King's ships (a merchantman, a packet, a fisherman and a privateer never had it), the challenge a flag at a named place by day and lights by night, the reply another. The actual tables are lost; the game's are drawn under the seed in the period's form, the same for every ship of one nation in one world. It is a paper of the cabin:

```orders cutter
the private signal
# rejected: show the private signal
# rejected: answer the private signal
```

The hired cutter of this book carries no table, and the orders are refused with the reason ("there is no private signal aboard: the month's table is issued to the King's ships under seal"); in a King's ship, a cruiser or a commodore's (and in any ship whose scenario gives `papers: {private_signals: true}`) they read:

```
The private signal: by day, a blue flag at the mizen topmast head; the reply, a chequered flag at the mizen topmast head, with the ensign at the ensign staff.
Bend on the signal: the private signal (a blue flag at the mizen topmast head; the table for June 1805).
```

A ship that holds another nation's table beside her own (an ally's carried among her papers, or one taken in a prize) may make that nation's challenge, or every table she holds one after another, as the *Java* made the English, Spanish and Portuguese private signals in succession to a strange frigate in 1812, and none was answered:

```orders cutter
# rejected: make the Spanish private signal
# rejected: make the private signals in succession
```

Each challenge is made in its own nation's form, a minute to run it up and three minutes for the answer before the next goes up in its place. A ship answers only the challenge made in her own nation's form, so the answer, or none, is judged against the table it was made from: "The frigate answers the Danish private signal rightly: a Danish ship"; "The frigate makes no answer to the private signals, British, Spanish and Portuguese in succession: a man of war under false colours, an enemy." A table the ship does not hold is refused in words that name the tables she does.

A King's ship's rules captain makes it to every strange sail within the flags' range once her colours are made out, and reads the answer from the minute his flags are up: right makes her one of his; a reply and the wrong one is the tell whatever her colours ("The cutter answers the private signal wrong: a stranger under false colours, an enemy"), and he gives chase by his doctrine; no answer under his own colours is the tell for a man of war's rig, and leaves a trader's rig "a merchantman, who has none; a stranger to be spoken". A ship with no table cannot answer; a privateer may bluff a guess, which is wrong by every table but her own.

## A signal made and read

The books are the nation's (`the signal book` among the ship's papers, `library(topic='papers', section='the signal book')`, which says whose it is: this ship's own by her service; Britain's the Admiralty's of 1799 with Popham's dictionary of 1803; the French by du Pavillon's table, two flags read as a cell; the Spanish Mazarredo's, two flags of twenty read as a cell of four hundred, with his lanterns by night and his guns in fog; the American Barry's, a flag a figure, the same numbers by lanterns at night; the Neapolitan the gunboat flotilla's book, its general signals by number; every other nation the common book every navy had, whose page says it is no nation's own and no other ship's numbers are to be read from it), and a signal is made by its meaning, never by a number you must know:

```orders cutter
make the signal to tack in succession
make the signal: enemy in sight bearing NW
make the signal No. 16
make the telegraphic signal: England expects that every man will do his duty
# rejected: answer the signal
# rejected: repeat the signal
haul down the signal
the signals
```

```
Bend on the signal: No. 54, tack in succession (No. 54, tack in succession, the British numerary book; a minute to run it up).
The signal is up: No. 54, tack in succession.
Bend on the signal: the telegraphic signal, England expects that every man will do his duty (12 hoists, 12 minutes, the telegraph flag over each: England 253, expects 269, that 863, every 261, man 471, will 958, do 220, his 370, D 4, U 21, T 19, Y 24; spelt letter by letter where the book has no word: DUTY).
```

`answer the signal` and `repeat the signal` want a signal read from another ship first, and are refused until one has been.

The hoist is an evolution: three hands at the halyards and a minute a hoist, a telegraphic sentence a minute a word, and it breaks out when the hands have run it up, not when you ordered it (a hoist queued behind a wear waits its hands). By night the same order makes the night book's lights and false fires; in fog the fog book's gun, fired by the battery, since the flags are not to be seen. The signal flies a glass at most and then comes down of itself. It flies at the main unless you name another place: `make the signal No. 16 at the fore`, `... at the main yardarm`; the lookout of the other ship says where ("two flags at the fore"). The browser's picture of your ship draws the hoist at its place, flag under flag down the halyard, the uppermost first, each flag drawn as your book's plates have it for the year (the British numerals changed in 1793, 1799 and 1803); it is gone when the hoist comes down. Another ship's hoist is told in words alone.

It is read at the other end by her lookout: "The brig-sloop of war makes a signal: two flags at the main" at the flags' range, and a minute later, if her library holds the book, "The brig-sloop of war's signal is No. 54, tack in succession"; without the book, "a signal we cannot read: not of our book". A rules captain who reads it hoists the answering pendant, and performs it when the sender is the ship he is under the orders of (the scenario's `under_the_orders_of`, or the director's word): a convoy of four merchantmen under your orders reads your `make the signal to tack in succession` from the convoy's book and puts about one after another, the leader first and each when the ship ahead of her has (`data/scenarios/convoy.yaml`). No ship obeys a signal of a ship she is not under the orders of; she logs it. Your own ship obeys nobody's: you read the signal and choose.

## A hail

Within four cables a ship is spoken by the trumpet, and the answer is a line heard on deck (the orders, which want a sail in sight within hail and so are not run through this book's empty sea):

```
hail her
hail the brig: what ship is that?
hail the brig what ship is that?
say to the brig: heave to, I am sending a boat
hail Nancy, whence come you?
```

The sail is named by her rig (`the brig`, `a topsail schooner`, `the fore topsail schooner`), by her rate (`the sixty-four`), by her name when it is known (`Nancy`, `the Nancy`), or as `her` for the sail engaged or the nearest; or as the lookout names her, by her kind with her quarter, her bearing and her distance (`hail the brig on the starboard bow: what ship is that?`, `hail the sail bearing SE by E, ho there`; chapter 15, "Naming a sail"), where two of a kind are near; capitals do not matter, the colon after her is optional, and what follows is said as written. A sail that has just hailed you is answered in the same words, whether or not your masthead has reported her yet. `hail <words>` with no sail named is the lookout's hail of the deck (chapter 19), and from anyone else it is refused in words; from the lookout every `hail` is his hail of the deck, whatever sail it reports ("hail The pilot's cutter is out of sight to the westward"), since the masthead speaks to no other ship.

```
Hailed the brig by the trumpet: 'What ship is that?'. The brig answers: 'The Nancy of Guernsey, from Guernsey for Falmouth, a cargo of salt.'
```

A sail that answers with her name is named by it in the readings of other sail (chapter 15) while she stays in sight, and may be named by it in every order after.

Beyond four cables the hail is refused with the distance. A rig word (`the lugger`, `the brig`) names a sail the lookout has in sight, never one beyond his sight on the world's sea; a sail's name reaches her wherever she is, and is refused for the distance; and a mark in sight that answers to the words (a wreck, a hulk riding to a sea-anchor) is said to be no sail, since nobody is aboard her to speak. A rules captain answers by the table in the period's form, by his role and his nation: a King's ship names herself ("His Majesty's cutter Sherbourne. And what ship is that?") and heaves to for nobody; a merchantman answers what ship, whence, whither, her cargo and the news, and heaves to for a King's ship not at war with her; a privateer under false colours lies under the flag she shows, with a name and a port of that nation; a model at her captain's station hears your words and answers in his own. A rules captain who comes within hail of you hails you first ("She hails: 'What ship is that?'"), and `say to her: His Majesty's cutter Sherbourne` is read for your nation by his table. Two rules captains within hail of one another exchange the standard questions once, written to the log in the lookout's hearing.

## The lights and the bell

By night a ship shows what her order and her book give her: a stern lantern for ships in company, the top lights of a squadron, a fishing boat's lantern at her nets, a merchantman often none; the lookout sees a light at three miles on a clear night beyond the hull's loom at the mile ("A light two points on the bow, bearing SW"). In fog a ship keeping the fog signals rings her bell every two minutes at anchor, fires a gun every half hour under way in company and beats the drum alone, and is heard beyond the visibility ("A bell heard in the fog on the larboard bow, bearing W").

```orders cutter
hoist the lights
hoist the top lights
show no lights
keep the fog signals
cease the fog signals
the lights
```

## The forms, in a table

| Order | What it does |
|---|---|
| `hoist the colours` | her own colours shown |
| `hoist French colours` / `hoist false colours` | another nation's (the principal enemy's when none is named): the ruse, until the first shot |
| `haul down the colours` | none shown; under fire, the strike |
| `hoist the ensign union down` | the signal of distress (with `fire a gun to leeward`) |
| `hoist the broad pendant` / `hoist a second ensign at the main` / `hoist the jack` | a commodore's broad pendant at the main; the ensigns doubled; the jack at the fore, or the jackstaff at anchor |
| `haul down the broad pendant` / `haul down the second ensign` / `haul down the jack` (or strike, lower) | each flag of command down by its name, the colours left flying |
| `the flags` | what flies where, and then the signal flying |
| `dress ship` / `undress ship` (or take down the dressing) | her flags and pendants hung in the yards, the stays and the shrouds in the fashion of the day; taken down, and down of themselves at sunset or with the colours |
| `show the private signal` / `answer the private signal` | the month's challenge and reply, with a table aboard |
| `make the Spanish private signal` / `make the private signals in succession` | another nation's challenge from her table held, or every table held one after another |
| `make the signal <meaning>` / `make the signal No. <n>` | a signal of the book by day, the night book's lights by night, the fog book's gun in fog; `... at the fore` (or the mizen, a masthead, a yardarm) flies it there, at the main else |
| `make the telegraphic signal: <words>` | Popham's free sentence, a hoist a word (a well-built book with a dictionary) |
| `answer the signal` / `repeat the signal` / `haul down the signal` | the answering pendant; the last signal read made again; the hoists down |
| `hoist the lights` / `hoist the top lights` / `show no lights` | the lights by night |
| `keep the fog signals` / `cease the fog signals` | the bell, the gun or the drum in fog |
| `hail her` / `hail the <sail>: <words>` / `hail the <sail> <words>` / `say to the <sail>: <words>` / `hail <name> <words>` | the trumpet within four cables; the colon optional, the sail by her rig, her rate, her name or `her` |
| `the colours`, `the signals`, `the private signal`, `the lights` | the readings |

Every signal of your ship's book is listed in `the signal book` of her papers, with its number, its meaning and its source; the hoists made up in the period's form rather than read from a book are marked so in the files, under `data/signals/`.
