# 27. The prize

Chapter 22 ended at the colours struck and chapter 25 at the deck carried. This chapter is what follows: the prize taken possession of, her prize crew and her prize master, her prisoners, the rising and the retaking, and the prize burnt or scuttled when she is not worth her men. Everything in it is an order, a reading or a line of the log of the game's own; where the period's practice is followed, the book that gives it is named. The prize court and the money, the captive's own channel and the fight heard off the horizon have chapters of their own as they come into the game, and the end of this one says what is not in it.

## Taking possession

A ship that has struck to you lies to for her captor with her colours down. She is nobody's until possession is taken of her, and a struck ship left lying may fill away again when you are out of gunshot, as the actions' narratives have more than one do. Possession is taken by the boat, within a boat's pull (a mile):

```
take possession of her
take possession of the Medusa
```

The boat crosses with a prize master and a prize crew, and the log says what was done in one line:

```
! Afternoon watch (14:12)  Possession is taken of the Medusa (struck by her colours): Mr Ward and twenty-two hands put aboard her as her prize crew, her people, two hundred and fifty, prisoners confined below. The colours hoisted over hers. She is for Falmouth, in company.
```

**When your boarders carry her deck**, the officer who stands on it takes possession of her at once, by the practice of every boarding action: he is her prize master and the boarders on her deck her first prize crew; there is no boat to send. When the boarders' officer falls in the fight, the senior standing of the party leads on and takes possession in his place, and the log names him ("Mr Ward leads the boarders on, their officer fallen, to a frigate's deck and has it").

**The prize crew** is a share of your fit men by the captain's doctrine (a King's ship spares about one man in twelve, a privateer rather more), never fewer than six, never more than half her own people nor a third of yours, drawn from the party on her deck first where there is one and from the watch on deck otherwise. **The prize master** is the first of the doctrine's ladder who can be spared: a lieutenant, a mate, a master's mate, a midshipman, a petty officer; the first lieutenant stays with his ship, as Falconer has the conqueror "invest his principal officer with the command of the prize" and the narratives have a lieutenant or a master's mate take her home. His station in her is her **captain's**, and he is at home in her from the taking: `where am I` asked of him says "aboard the Medusa, on the quarterdeck", and his own ship's station is kept for his return.

**Her people are her prisoners**, confined below under the prize crew's guard: her seamen in the hold, her officers in the cabin, where the game's rule of the places keeps them (chapter 26's rule of the visitor turned about: a prisoner reads what his place gives him and no more). Her captain's command passes to the prize master; her colours are yours, hoisted over hers for a day as the practice was and then alone; her goal is your doctrine's, the nearest port open to your nation (Falmouth or Plymouth from the Channel for a King's ship, home for a privateer who has one), and her intent is her prize master's: "carry the prize to Falmouth". She keeps your company while she is bound the same way, as a member of your set (chapter 24), unless your doctrine parts from her at once, as a privateer's does; the line says which.

A ship that cannot spare a prize crew does not take possession: the refusal says so, and a prize not worth her men is burnt (below).

## The readings

| reading | what it says |
|---|---|
| `the prize`, `the prizes` | every prize of this ship's: her name, how she was taken, her prize master, the prize crew's and the prisoners' counts, her goal, in company or parted, and where she lies by the lookout; "no prize" |
| `the prisoners`, `the prisoners aboard` | the prisoners this ship holds: how many, how many of them hurt, where confined, in irons, the named with their parole; "no prisoners" |
| `the prize crew` | on a prize: the men who work her, how many fit and how tired, the hours since the taking, the prize master; on any other ship "she is no prize" |

```
The prize: the Medusa, carried by boarding, under Mr Ward with a prize crew of twenty-two and two hundred and fifty prisoners, for Falmouth, in company, bearing SSW, 0.3 miles.
The prisoners: four prisoners, confined below.
```

A prize's prisoners say "two hundred and fifty prisoners, fifty-five of them hurt, confined below"; her surgeon is among them and dresses them as he would his own, for the sick list is his whatever flag flies.

## The prisoners

A prize carried whole has more prisoners in her than prize crew to guard them, which is the danger of every prize in the narratives. **Take them into your own hold** by the boat, so many at a time, while she lies within a boat's pull:

```
take the prisoners aboard
take six of the prisoners aboard
take twenty of the prisoners aboard from the Medusa
```

Each boat's load is a line in the log ("Six of the Medusa's people taken aboard by the boat and confined in the hold; two hundred and forty-four left in her"), and `the prisoners` on your own ship counts them from then. Prisoners are victualled as your own people are, at the rate the Regulations give, and they eat your water and your bread; a captain who takes two hundred into a frigate already on short allowance will hear of it from the purser's readings.

**The rising.** Prisoners left in a prize under a small or a tired prize crew may rise on them: once an hour, by night or in weather, when their fit men are twice the prize crew's and the prize crew is worn, the game draws for it by the same tables as the fight on deck (chapter 25). Put down, they are in irons for a day and the log says so; won, the prize is **retaken by her own people**, her colours hers again, her prize master and his men her prisoners, and your log says "the Medusa retaken by her prisoners" when the lookout sees her colours change or the news comes by another road. The guard against it is the practice's: divide the prisoners between the two ships, keep the prize in company, and do not leave a worn prize crew in her for days.

**Retaken by a cruiser.** A prize parted from you, alone with six men in her, may fall in with a man-of-war of her own nation; she yields to him as any merchantman yields to what she cannot fight, and she is his, your prize crew his prisoners. The line comes to you when you learn of it, not before.

## The prize burnt or scuttled

A prize that is not worth her prize crew, a poor coaster when your own people are thin, is **burnt or scuttled** by the practice of every cruise in the narratives, her people taken aboard as prisoners and the prize crew home again:

```
burn the prize
burn the Medusa
scuttle the prize
```

```
! Afternoon watch (15:40)  The Medusa is burnt: her people, two hundred and forty-six, taken aboard as prisoners, the prize crew home again; nothing is to be had for her at any court but the head money, if she was a ship of war.
```

The captain's doctrine burns of itself what it will not man (`burns_when: not worth manning`), and the log says so in the same words.

## The events

For the standing book and the stand-by (chapter 16): `she is taken` (possession taken of a prize, on the captor), `possession taken` (on the prize herself), `the prisoners aboard` (a boat's load of them), `the prisoners rising`, `the prize retaken`, `a prize lost` (retaken, by her people or by a cruiser, when you learn of it) and `a prize burnt`.

```
standing order "the prize": at she is taken then take the prisoners aboard
```

## What is not in this chapter

The prize court and the prize money (the condemnation, the eighths, the head money and the agent's commission), the captive's own channel when the person you hold is a captain with a voice of his own, and the fight heard off the horizon are told in the chapters that follow as they come into the game; the readings and the orders of this chapter are the game's now.
