INTRODUCTION. 


X H I S Vocabulary  was  originally  made  to  facilitate  the  convey- 
ance of  messages  from  the  Romney  off  Copenhagen,  to  Admiral 
Dickson  off  Elsineur ; its  utility  was  in  that  instance  so  obvious, 
and  so  generally  allowed  by  the  Captains  of  the  North  Sea  squa- 
dron, that  Sir  Home  Popham  conceived  it  might  be  brought  into 
more  extensive  practice,  and  has  already  given  copies  to  different 
officers,  with  the  request,  that  they  will  point  out  any  improvement, 
and  send  it  to  Lord  Spencer,  who  has  the  original  for  consideration 
how  far  it  may  be  expedient  to  print  it,  and  bring  it  forward  for 
general  use. 


It  is  by  no  means  intended  to  interfere  with  the  established  sig- 
nals, as  a single  signal  is  certainly  the  most  efficient  for  military 
evolutions. 

It  frequently  however  happens  that  officers  wish  to  make  com- 
munications of  very  essential  moment  far  beyond  the  capacity  of 
the  established  signals,  and  it  is  presumed  that  this  Vocabulary  will 
afford  such  convenience.

Words  of  more  general  use  than  those  already  established  can  at 
all  times  be  inserted,  especially  those  of  local  signification  on  diffe- 
rent services ; and  words  may  always  be  changed,  or  others  added, 
without  any  intercourse  except  by  the  Vocabulary. 

Orders  may  be  communicated  for  ships  to  go  on  detached  ser- 
vices, when  it  would  be  impossible  to  hoist  a boat  out,  or  have  any 
communication  with  the  Commander  in  Chief : and  secret  keys 
numbered  to  be  opened  according  to  signals,  may  be  established  to 
communicate  secret  orders.  By  the  addition  of  a pendant,  this 
Vocabulary  may  be  carried  to  a great  extent,  and  by  having  the 
words  of  an  Entick  or  other  small  dictionary  numbered,  it  will  be 
as  comprehensive  as  a dictionary ; though  I think  it  is  at  present 
sufficiently  so  for  all  general  purposes  at  sea. 

This  Vocabulary  may  be  also  used  as  a cipher,  either  in  the  com- 
mon or  secret  keys,  but  the  secret  keys  and  their  changes  must  be 
fixed  on  by  the  Commander  in  Chief. 


Plymouth,  April  18,  1803. 

1 experienced  so  many  advantages  from  the  use  of  these  signals, 
that  on  my  return  to  England  from  the  Red  Sea,  I prepared  a se- 
cond edition,  and  the  favourable  opinion  which  several  officers  of 
the  squadrdn  at  Plymouth  expressed  of  the  utility  of  the  first  edi- 
tion induced  me  to  reprint  it,  with  two  more  parts,  of  words  and 
sentences.

Instances  of  the  impossibility  of  making  communications  by 
trumpet  in  heavy  gales  of  wind,  when  it  is  of  the  last  importance  to 
convey  intelligence,  have  happened  so  frequently,  that  it  is  unne- 
cessary to  recapitulate  them. 

The  general  port  signals  may  also  be  expeditiously  communi- 
cated from  the  Commander  in  Chief’s  ship,  by  which  the  wear  and 
tear  of  boats  will  be  materially  saved,  and  the  necessity  of  their 
bearing  up  for  Portsmouth  harbour  in  bad  weather  obviated.  When 
this  is  the  case  the  boats  frequently  get  very  much  damaged  and  the 
crews  desert,  or  get  ill  by  lying  in  their  boats  with  their  wet  clothes 
on;  as  officers  do  not  always  carry  them  on  board  the  ships  in  har- 
bour, and  it  is  even  well  to  avoid  that  communication. 

The  second  and  third  parts  make  the  Vocabulary  very  compre- 
hensive, and  their  application  is  as  simple  as  the  first  part. 

The  second  part  consists  of  words  next  useful  to  those  in  the  first, 
and  is  placed  opposite  to  it  in  alphabetical  order,  to  save  the  trou- 
ble of  opening  the  book  twice. 

The  third  part  is  placed  by  itself,  as  it  consists  of  sentences  most 
applicable  to  military  or  general  conversation.  These  sentences 
should  be  frequently  practised  to  make  them  familiar,  as  they  will 
save  many  signals.

General  Observation's. 

The  flags  are  intended  to  represent  the  figures  placed  opposite 
to  them  in  the  annexed  table.  A flag  hoisted  alone,  or  under 
another  flag,  is  to  represent  units.  When  two  flags  are  hoisted, 
the  upper  flag  is  to  represent  tens.  When  three  arc  hoisted,  the 
uppermost  is  to  represent  hundreds,  the  next  tens,  and  the  lowest 
units.  The  thousands  are  denoted  by  pendants  or  balls,  as  may 
appear  most  likely  to  be  seen,  inferior  and  superior:  superior,  1000; 
inferior,  2000.  As  for  example: 

No.  1026.  Aback.  No.  2026.  Shall  I leave  off  action  ? 


When  the  substitute  flag  is  hoisted  under  other  flags,  it  is  to  re- 
present the  same  figure  as  the  flag  immediately  above  it.  For 
example  : — to  represent  the  number  33,  the  substitute  flag  will  be 
hoisted  under  the  flag  representing  3.  When  the  cipher  flag  is 
hoisted  under  other  flags,  it  represents  the  cipher,  as  the  cipher 
flag  under  number  3 represents  30. 

Prepositions  and  articles  will  be  used  as  seldom  as  possible,  and 
the  sentences  will  be  made  short.  In  verbs,  the  number,  person, 
tense,  and  mood,  must  be  applied  to  the  sense  of  the  sentence ; 
and  attention  in  the  same  respect  must  be  paid  to  words  having 
more  than  one  termination,  and  to  numbers  in  general.  But  when 
it  happens  that  the  exact  word  is  not  in  the  vocabulary,  one

Simple  words  may  be  used  to  form  a compound,  which  will

Compass  signals  may  be  used  in  messages,  if  necessary,  as  their 
flags  are  not  in  the  numerical  code.
