# Rig geometry and canvas: research notes for milestone 3b

Read by the build session on 2026-09-26 from the texts under this folder, in preparation for `docs/TechnicalSpec-M3b.md`. Citations give the work, the section or article, and the approximate line in the OCR file for finding the passage again; quote the page, not the line, in data files. Numbers here are as printed; where the OCR is doubtful it says so.

## 1. How sharp the yards can be braced

**Fincham 1843, *A Treatise on Masting Ships*, "General Observations" arts. 94 to 103 (OCR lines 5240 to 5540).** The one period-adjacent source with measured angles.

- **Measured, Hardy's experimental squadron of 1827** (art. 102, the note; the ships were the *Sapphire*, *Challenger* and *Tyne* of 28 guns and four 18-gun sloops): the long, fine ships, "by bracing their main-yard from 23° to 29°, and their fore-yards from 26° to 30°", could seldom lie within six points of the wind (the digit is lost in the OCR; six fits the sentence); the short ship, "by bracing her main-yard from 17° to 21°, and fore-yard from 25° to 28°, could sometimes lie within 5 points of the wind, and constantly gain to windward". Angles are between yard and keel; our ship files measure from square, so 23° from the keel is 67° from square.
- **Free and large** (art. 103): wind abeam, fore yards about 44°, main about 42° from the keel; a point or two abaft the beam, fore about 52°, main about 48°. Don Juan (d'Ulloa): 28° 47' close-hauled with all sail; 50° 11' large; 56° 21' with sail reduced.
- **Rules of practice** (arts. 94, 96, 98, 99, 101): the after yards are braced sharper than the head yards on a wind, so that the wind, bent forward by the head sails, strikes all the sails at the same angle (art. 94); with a sea on the weather bow or a ship full forward, the head yards are braced *less* sharp, or with much way and a griping ship *sharper* (art. 96); yards may be braced sharper as the press of sail increases, fast and clean ships sharper than full ones, small ships sharper than large, and "the flatter the sails the sharper they may be braced" (art. 98); seamen brace as sharp as possible and keep one point of the sail lifting, which is proper only with five or six knots of way, otherwise the sails should be kept full (art. 99); the wind on the sails should be a point and a half to two points more open than the yards' angle with the keel (art. 101).
- **The limit is the rigging** (art. 100, note): "The shrouds, stays, and other causes connected with the rigging, will seldom allow the yards of square-rigged vessels to be braced sufficiently sharp." Fore-and-aft sails lie at 13° to 17° to the keel; experienced cutter sailors keep the boom at 13° in light breezes, 15° in moderate, 17° in strong, the gaff 2° to 4° more open (art. 102 and note).
- **Leeway by brace angle, Euler** (art. 95): yards at 85° to the keel, leeway 1° 37' for long ships and 4° 36' for short; at 25°, 8° 1' and 21° 43'; "which agrees very nearly with the limits assigned by experienced seamen"; two to three points close-hauled in some ships, 8° to 12° in long fast ships in smooth water. Leeway grows when sail is reduced in a rising wind because the hull's windage counts for more (art. 95), and a ship that carried weather helm then carries it slack (art. 95).
- The Bouguer and Euler tables that follow (art. 103) are garbled in the OCR beyond use; the page image is needed if they are ever wanted.

**What this settles.** The brace limit stays a per-yard number in the ship file, as now, but its source becomes Fincham's measured angles by class of ship (a long frigate's lower yards about 23° to 29° from the keel, that is 61° to 67° from square; a short handy ship 17° to 21°, that is 69° to 73°), rather than Luce's paraphrase of them. Deriving the limit from channel breadth and shroud spread is not possible from the sources in hand: none of Steel, Lever, Fincham, Kipping or Luce gives channel breadths, which live in shipbuilding texts (Steel's *Naval Architecture* of 1805, Fincham's *Ship-building*) and on draughts. Recorded as future research; the owner offered to help read plans and model photographs when it is taken up.

## 2. The period device for bracing sharper: catharpins and swifters

**Steel 1794, vol. I, glossary "CATHARPINS" (line 19433):** "Short ropes, to keep the lower shrouds in tight, after they are braced in by [the swifter]"; the catharpin legs are four, the foremost shortest (line 21637); the shrouds are bowsed in "till the shrouds come into the length of the catharpin legs" (line 22355). **Lever 1808 (1827 printing), fig. 182 (lines 3528 to 3538):** a swifter is rove through tail-blocks on each shroud "that is to be catharpined in", the fall led across the deck, "the shrouds are then bowsed in", and the legs seized; the foremost shroud was formerly never catharpined in because it lies abreast the mast, but the merchant service now catharpins both foremost and aftermost.

The purpose, stated in every seamanship of the period and implied by Fincham's note, is to draw the lower shrouds in below the top so that the lower yards can be braced sharper before the lee yardarm and the sail foul the rigging. The cost is that the shrouds, drawn in, support the mast athwartships less well.

**What this settles.** The "slack the lee rigging" idea from the gate M2 review becomes the period practice: *swifter in the catharpins* (a few degrees more brace on that mast's lower yard, the mast rated down athwartships until eased), and *ease the catharpins* as the counter. Both are boatswain's work with the watch. Hardy's "measures" in the short ship are not named; catharpins and possibly shifted chain-plates are the likely ones, and the game needs only the one.

## 3. Bowlines

- **Fitting:** courses and topsails carry two bowline cringles on each leech, with bridles (Kipping 1898, *Sails and Sailmaking*, lines 10329, 10911); the frigate file already has bowlines on the courses only.
- **Use:** on a wind the weather bowlines are hauled to keep the weather leech taut so the sail stands closer without lifting; "haul taut the weather brace and haul the bowline" (Luce 1884, line 28443); "steady out the bowlines" is the order when filling and when braced up (Luce lines 24322, 29720); they are let go before tacking ("let go the bowlines" at the helm going down) and slacked when the yards are braced in. Steel 1794 vol. II uses the bowline in casting and in backing (lines 6900, 8638 to 8692).
- **Effect:** Fincham's "the flatter the sails the sharper they may be braced" (art. 98) is the physics: a taut weather leech is a flatter sail, worth a few degrees at the luff.

**What this settles.** Bowlines on courses and topsails as parts (the topsails' to be added to the files with their gear), a `hauled` state that flattens the sail (its luff angle a few degrees closer) while braced up, let go by the tack and wear scripts and slacked when braced in, hands from the forecastlemen and afterguard. The owner's ruling: bowlines are in.

## 4. Canvas by sail, in period

**Steel 1794, vol. I, "Sailmaking" (lines 11045 to 12784), for the navy:** main and fore courses No. 1 or 2; mizzen topsail No. 4, 5 or 6; mizzen topgallant No. 7; royals No. 8; main staysail No. 1 to 3; mizzen staysail No. 2 or 3; main topmast staysail No. 5; fore topmast staysail No. 6; middle staysail No. 6; mizzen topmast staysail No. 7; main topgallant staysail No. 7. (The fore and main topsail and topgallant lines were not found in the OCR; Luce's table below supplies them.) **Kipping 1898 (the 1847 text), lines 11253 to 12192:** a course of No. 2 lined with No. 5; topmast studding sails No. 6, topgallant studding sails No. 7 or 8; lower studding sails No. 4 or 5. **Luce 1884, ch. X p. 171, read from the page (`Tables.md`):** the working suit No. 2 for courses, topsails, jib and spanker, mizzen topsail No. 3, topgallants Nos. 4 and 6, royals Nos. 8 and 9, flying jib No. 5, and every storm sail No. 1, with one foresail and the fore and main topsails of the pair made of No. 1 for heavy weather. **Strength by number** from Luce App. E (`Tables.md`): No. 1 bears 470 lb crosswise on an inch strip, No. 2 420, falling to 224 for No. 9; weight per bolt 84 lb down to 34.

**What this settles.** Every sail gets a `canvas_no` in the ship file by a rule the generator can cite (courses and topsails 2, mizzen topsail 3, topgallants 4 and 6, royals 8 and 9, jib 2, flying jib 5, staysails 3 to 7 by height, lower studding sails 5, topmast 6, topgallant 7, storm canvas 1), a cloth rating from the number by Appendix E's strengths, and a heavy-weather foresail and topsails of No. 1 in the sail room.

## 5. Studding sails and the wind

**Luce 1866 ch. XXIII (line 26509) and 1884 ch. XXIII (line 28668):** studding sails are for light or moderate weather with the wind free or aft; the weather topmast, topgallant and royal studding sails may be set with the wind one point free, seven points from the keel; the lower studding sail only with the wind abaft the beam; both sides with the wind aft and the yards square. They are taken in before going about (the tacking sequences in both editions begin with them in). **Steel 1794 vol. I:** the lower studding sail boom is half the main yard; topmast studding sail booms are half the length of the yard they go on (lines 4555 to 4559); the booms run through irons on the yardarms (lines 4189, 4265).

**What this settles.** The rule is met through the physics where the physics can carry it: the studding sail class's curve stalls and the sail flogs forward of its angle, the boom is geometry that can foul the lee rigging when the yard is braced beyond a point, and the strain model already prices flogging. The one hard rule is that a sail cannot be set on a boom that is in. Booms start rigged in, as at sea.

## 6. Adjacent yards

Nothing in the sources gives a number for how far one yard may be braced from the next on the same mast; the sources brace by mast ("brace up the fore yards") and the constraint is physical: the topsail's foot against the lower yard's lifts and the course's head, the upper braces against the yardarms below. Fincham's art. 94 gives the *intended* difference between masts, not between yards. So the clearance is computed from the parts (yard lengths, heights and the sail between them), not looked up.

## 7. Gaps and further research

- **Channel breadths and shroud spread** for a geometric brace limit: Steel's *Naval Architecture* (1805), draughts, model photographs. Not needed for 3b.
- **The Bouguer and Euler tables** in Fincham art. 103, from the page image, if a leeway-by-angle truth is ever wanted beyond Euler's four numbers.
- **Steel's spar tables by rate** (vol. I, line 5208 onward: masts and yards for 38 down to 20 guns and sloops) are in period and could replace Luce's rules in the generator; the OCR is rough and the page image would be needed.
- **Fincham's arts. 58 and 59** place the axis of the sails 0.05 of the beam (0.038 for the mizzen) before the mast when braced up, and note the sail plane lies about a yard's diameter before the mast: useful when the view draws sails against masts.

## 8. Ringtails, water sails and save-alls: whose sails they were

Asked by the owner before the sails were placed. **Falconer 1780, RING-TAIL (line 15666):** a ship's ring-tail is "a small triangular sail, extended on a little mast, which is occasionally erected for that purpose on the top of a ship's stern", its foot on a boom projecting horizontally from the stern, "only used in light and favourable winds, particularly in the Atlantic ocean"; the other kind borders the after leech of a boom mainsail "as in all sloops, brigs, and schooners", and is "of the same depth with that part of the main-sail upon which it borders". **Steel 1794 vol. I:** "Sloop's ringtail sail ... canvas No. 5 or 8 ... occasionally hoisted abaft the mainsail" (line 15455); the ringtail boom "lashed occasionally to the outer end of the main-sail-boom" (line 19295); "Sloop's water-sail" and "some ships have a water-sail, similar to a sloop's water-sail" (lines 15033 to 15040); a "sloop's save-all topsail", a small square sail under the topsail (lines 533, 14969). **Kipping 1847 (line 26611):** a brig's ringtail "sets like a topmast studding sail, outside of the after-leech of the main-trysail", with a sliding-gunter ringtail boom, No. 5 or 6 canvas. **Luce 1884 (line 29072):** "a ring-tail, which sets abaft the spanker; a save-all, under the lower studding-sail boom ... never met with in the service now", that is, carried earlier; and in the yachting appendix water sails "usually triangular, may be set under the spinnaker boom".

So: the ringtail is period on ships (a driver's ringtail, Falconer) and on fore-and-aft vessels alike; the water sail is a sloop's and schooner's sail that some ships copied; the save-all under a studding sail boom is a ship's sail, and the save-all topsail a cutter's. Placement in spec 3b §6.4 follows.
